# Visual Reference: Earth's Atmospheric Limb from High Orbits

**Date:** 2026-03-22
**Purpose:** Research reference for rendering the atmospheric limb (blue haze, airglow) in Sunlit Earth, focusing on what it actually looks like from distances where the full disk is visible — geostationary orbit through lunar distance.

---

## Angular Scale Context

Before reviewing the photos, it helps to understand the geometry. The scale is key to understanding why the atmosphere looks very different across distance regimes.

Earth's visible atmosphere spans roughly 100 km altitude (for Rayleigh scattering) to about 80–100 km for the brightest airglow layer. Using the small-angle approximation (angle ≈ size / distance, converted to degrees):

| Vantage point | Distance from Earth center | Atmosphere thickness (arc) | Earth disk diameter (arc) | Atmosphere as % of disk radius |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ISS | ~6,771 km | ~0.85° | ~109° | ~1.6% |
| Gemini 11 / Polaris Dawn | ~7,774 km | ~0.74° | ~95° | ~1.6% |
| Geostationary (GEO) | ~42,164 km | ~0.136° | ~17.4° | ~1.6% |
| Lunar distance | ~384,400 km | ~0.015° (0.9 arcmin) | ~1.9° | ~1.6% |
| DSCOVR / L1 | ~1,500,000 km | ~0.0038° (0.23 arcmin) | ~0.49° | ~1.6% |

The critical insight: **the atmosphere's angular thickness relative to Earth's disk diameter stays constant at all distances** (both scale inversely with distance). What changes is whether that thin rim is resolvable at all given image resolution and contrast. At ISS altitude you are close enough that even a rough sensor captures the limb prominently. At lunar distance the entire blue rim subtends less than 1 arcminute of arc — still physically there, but razor-thin.

## 1. ISS (400 km) — The Reference Baseline

**Mission/source:** Hundreds of ISS expeditions, primarily limb photography by astronauts; also ICON satellite at ~580 km.

### Daytime atmospheric limb (looking edge-on)

From ISS altitude (~400 km), the atmosphere is famously layered and colorful when viewed edge-on at the limb. Descriptions from NASA Earth Observatory and astronaut accounts:

- **Bottom band — troposphere (orange to red-orange):** The lowest, densest layer glows orange to red because dust, smoke, and aerosols scatter shorter wavelengths; this band appears orange-ish even in daylight. Extends to roughly 12–20 km altitude. Multiple dark cloud layers are visible within it.
- **Transition zone — tropopause (brownish/darker band):** A thin darker transition marks the top of the troposphere.
- **Middle band — stratosphere (milky white to faint pink):** Mostly featureless; occasionally noctilucent clouds visible at 80 km if conditions allow.
- **Upper band — mesosphere/thermosphere (blue to black gradient):** Shades of blue grading into the blackness of space. This blue arises from Rayleigh scattering in the very thin upper atmosphere.

From the ISS perspective the atmosphere occupies a substantial fraction of the view when looking sideways — the orange troposphere alone looks like a thick glowing band. Astronaut Ulf Merbold (STS-9, 1983) described the horizon as "accentuated by a thin seam of dark blue light: the atmosphere."

### Nightglow / airglow from ISS

Airglow is one of the most visually spectacular atmospheric features visible from ISS, but it is a nighttime or twilight phenomenon requiring long camera exposures:

- **Green airglow band:** The dominant and brightest feature. Peak emission altitude 90–100 km (roughly 60 miles), where excited oxygen atoms (O) emit at 557.7 nm (yellow-green). Appears as a bright, narrow, continuous green arc along the entire limb when viewed edge-on.
- **Red airglow:** OH radical emissions and atomic oxygen at higher altitudes (~200–300 km); dimmer than green, appears reddish above the green band.
- **Orange/yellow airglow:** Sodium D-line emission at ~90 km from sodium atoms; sometimes visible as a thin golden-orange band.
- **Vertical structure:** In ISS photos with long exposures, the nightglow typically appears as 2–3 stacked color bands: a bright thin green band at ~90–100 km, sometimes a dimmer red band above it, with darker blue transition zones between.

Critically, airglow is an **edge-on phenomenon from orbit.** When viewed from the ISS looking perpendicular to the limb, you are integrating the emission along a long column of atmosphere (thousands of km of path length), which makes the thin ~30 km thick layer appear bright. If you looked straight down, airglow would be invisible against the Earth's surface because the column path is too short.

**Key characteristic:** In ISS nightglow photos, the green band looks like a distinct, relatively sharp luminescent arc — not a fuzzy haze, but a well-defined glowing layer sitting above the darker troposphere. Its angular thickness from ISS is roughly 0.5–0.8° — comparable to the Moon's diameter in Earth's sky. It is prominent and unmistakable in long-exposure photography.

## 2. Gemini 11 (1,374 km) and Polaris Dawn (1,408 km) — Medium-High Orbit

**Missions:** Gemini 11 (September 1966, Richard Gordon and Pete Conrad) reached 1,374 km — the human spaceflight altitude record at the time. Polaris Dawn (September 2024, Jared Isaacman crew) reached 1,408 km, briefly exceeding Gemini 11's record.

Both missions remained in low enough orbit that Earth still dominates the view — roughly 95° across — rather than showing a full disk. At 1,374 km the observer is still embedded within the outer ionosphere.

**Gemini 11:** The mission carried an explicit "Airglow Horizon Photography" experiment designed to measure the altitudes of atomic oxygen and sodium layers by direct photography. This confirms that airglow was detectable and scientifically useful at this altitude. At 1,374 km, the green airglow layer at 90–100 km is viewed at a more oblique angle than from the ISS, but the geometry still permits edge-on limb observations.

**Polaris Dawn (2024):** The crew captured a widely-shared image described as Earth's "brilliant blue limb against the blackness of space." From this altitude Earth still filled a large portion of the view, and the blue atmospheric haze at the limb was described as prominent and distinctly visible. Coverage focused on the mission's historic nature rather than atmospheric analysis, but the blue limb is clearly visible in released imagery. One crew summary: "from here, Earth sure looks like a perfect world." The Polaris Dawn images show an orbital perspective approximately 3x higher than ISS, where Earth's curvature is more pronounced but the full disk is not yet visible.

**Inference for airglow:** At ~1,400 km, the observer is well above the airglow layer (90–100 km). The edge-on geometry for limb viewing is still favorable. Airglow would remain photographically detectable in long-exposure nightside images, but the angular scale of the green band relative to the field of view is smaller than from the ISS.

## 3. Geostationary Orbit (35,786 km) — Himawari, GOES, Meteosat

**Missions:** GOES-16/17/18 (NOAA), Himawari-8/9 (JMA, Japan), Meteosat-10/11 (EUMETSAT). All operate from ~36,000 km altitude above the equator.

From GEO, Earth subtends approximately 17.4° — showing roughly 40% of the sphere's surface area. Earth is recognizably a disk but not the full globe familiar from the Blue Marble; it looks more like a large flattened sphere with continents and weather clearly resolved.

### Daytime limb from GEO

**The blue atmospheric limb is visible in GEO imagery, but it is thin.** EUMETSAT's "Natural Colour Enhanced" Meteosat imagery shows a subtle blue atmospheric softening at the limb. Key observations from researchers and image analysts:

- The edge of Earth appears slightly softened and diffuse rather than geometrically sharp. This is the combined effect of Rayleigh scattering, ozone absorption, and aerosol scattering in the atmosphere.
- A gradual blue brightening occurs approaching the limb, but it is not a bold colored band as seen from ISS — it is a subtle haze rim.
- Atmospheric haze "increases as you approach the edge of the disk" — this is an observer-consistent description from multiple sources examining GEO full-disk imagery.
- In GEO natural-color imagery, the atmosphere at the very edge can appear as a thin softened blue-gray transition zone rather than the vivid stratified bands visible from low orbit.

**Himawari-8** (Japan's AHI camera, 500 m visible resolution) sometimes captures the moon passing behind Earth; close analysis reveals significant atmospheric refraction at the limb during these lunar occultation events. The atmosphere bends and distorts the Moon's edge into a "flange" or "crown" of stretched light — this shows the atmosphere is optically present and refracting at GEO distances, even if it does not appear as a bold colored rim in standard true-color images.

### Airglow from GEO

**Standard visible-light airglow is not detectable in GEO imagery.** The reason is both geometric and radiometric:

- From 36,000 km, the 90–100 km airglow layer is viewed at a much less favorable edge-on angle. The effective path length through the emission layer from a face-on perspective is far shorter than from low orbit.
- Airglow is intrinsically faint — roughly one-tenth the brightness of all stars combined in the night sky — and requires long exposures even from 400 km. From 36,000 km the signal per pixel would be orders of magnitude lower.
- Standard weather satellite cameras are designed for reflected sunlight (dayside), not faint emissions.

**However, airglow IS detectable from geostationary orbit with purpose-built UV instruments.** NASA's GOLD mission (Global-scale Observations of the Limb and Disk) rides on the SES-14 commercial communications satellite in GEO above 47.5°W. GOLD observes airglow at far-ultraviolet wavelengths (132–162 nm), measuring emissions from atomic oxygen (135.6 nm) and molecular nitrogen LBH bands. These emissions are invisible to the human eye and standard cameras — GOLD needs a specialized UV spectrograph. GOLD images show the ionosphere's structure and dynamics — but these are false-color UV maps, not anything visible to the eye or a conventional camera from GEO.

**Conclusion for GEO:** A standard color camera at GEO would show a thin softened blue-gray atmospheric rim at the very edge of Earth's disk in true-color imagery. It is present but subtle — a haze effect rather than a bold colored arc. Airglow is completely invisible to any visible-light camera at this distance and requires dedicated UV instrumentation.

## 4. Apollo Missions — 29,000–400,000 km

### Apollo 17 "Blue Marble" (December 7, 1972 — ~29,400 km)

**Camera:** Hasselblad with 80 mm Zeiss lens, 70 mm film.

Taken about five hours after launch, while Apollo 17 was en route to the Moon at approximately 45,000 km from Earth (some sources cite 29,400 km from the surface — the discrepancy may reflect different measurement conventions). At this distance Earth fills a substantial portion of the frame and shows its full disk — Africa, Antarctica, the Indian Ocean, and cloud systems are all visible.

**Atmosphere description:** Multiple accounts describe the same visual effect:

- The atmosphere appears as "only a tiny sliver on the limb (horizon) of the earth" — the atmospheric rim is thin but distinctly visible as a haze zone at the edges of the disk.
- The limb is described as "sharp except for the lower right portion of the image where it is only slightly blurred" — this blurring is the atmospheric limb haze.
- The photo has become an iconic emblem of Earth's fragility specifically because the atmosphere appears so thin — barely distinguishable from the edge of the planet.
- In true color, the atmosphere at the limb appears as a pale blue-white haze, continuous around the disk's visible edge. It is photographically detectable but occupies a very thin zone.

**At ~45,000 km, the atmosphere subtends roughly 0.127° (about 7.6 arcminutes)** at its 100 km depth — still resolvable on 70 mm film through an 80 mm lens (film resolution adequate). The blue limb is real and photographically captured, but it is clearly a thin rim rather than a substantial band.

### Apollo 8 "Earthrise" (December 24, 1968 — ~384,000 km)

**Camera:** Hasselblad with color film, taken from lunar orbit.

The Earthrise photo captures Earth half-illuminated, rising over the lunar surface horizon from lunar orbit (approximately 384,000 km from Earth). Earth appears as a small, vivid blue-and-white marble.

**Atmosphere at lunar distance:** Consistent descriptions across multiple sources:

- Earth appears as "a bright blue-and-white marble hovering in the stark black vastness of space" with "marbled beauty" — the key feature is the whole-disk color, not a distinguishable atmospheric rim.
- The limb appears sharp in the Earthrise photograph — "limb is sharp" is the consistent description, with the atmospheric haze merging into the background at this scale.
- The atmosphere is not described as a separately distinguishable rim in the Earthrise image. The thin blue atmospheric haze is present physically but at ~384,000 km it is optically merged with the planet's own coloring.
- At lunar distance Earth subtends approximately 1.9° (just under 2°). The 100 km atmosphere layer subtends only about 0.017° (about 1 arcminute). On 70 mm Hasselblad film with an 80 mm lens, this approaches the resolution limit and would not appear as a distinct colored band — it blends into the edge.

**What IS visible from lunar distance:** The overall blue color of the disk (from the atmospheric Rayleigh scattering across the face of the planet), white clouds, brown land, and blue-white ice. The atmosphere makes the whole disk blue-tinged but does not show as a distinct rim at this scale. No airglow is visible in standard photography from lunar distance.

## 5. MESSENGER Earth Flyby (August 2, 2005)

**Mission:** MESSENGER (MErcury Surface, Space ENvironment, GEochemistry, and Ranging), NASA Mercury orbiter. Camera: MDIS (Mercury Dual Imaging System), wide and narrow angle CCD cameras.

**Closest approach:** 2,347 km over central Mongolia, then retreated over 24 hours. A 358-frame movie documents the full rotation as MESSENGER moved from 65,598 km to 435,885 km away.

**Descriptions of what was captured:**

- The true-color image shows "the spinning blue-green marble" with the Amazon jungle in "bright green" at center and West African deserts visible at the edge.
- A near-infrared image reveals vegetation in vivid red.
- At closest approach (~2,347 km) the geometry is similar to Gemini/Polaris Dawn — close enough that the blue atmospheric limb would be clearly visible as a distinct rim.
- As MESSENGER retreated, it captured Earth as a "receding crescent" — during crescent phases (large phase angle), the atmospheric limb becomes more prominent because you are looking at the sunlit edge at a grazing angle, which enhances limb brightening and atmospheric scattering.

**MESSENGER significance for airglow:** The MESSENGER flyby was at a distance regime where airglow at the limb would be photographically accessible in long exposures (similar to ISS distances at closest approach). The mission's MDIS cameras were calibrated for planetary surface photography rather than faint atmospheric emission, so dedicated airglow imaging was not a primary goal. However, MESSENGER did carry instruments for atmospheric composition sensing during the flyby.

## 6. Galileo Earth Flyby (December 1990)

**Mission:** Galileo spacecraft en route to Jupiter; two Earth gravity assists (December 1990 and December 1992). Camera: SSI (Solid State Imager), multispectral CCD.

**Key image set:** 500-frame time-lapse movie showing 25 hours of Earth rotation, taken December 11, 1990, at distances ranging from roughly 2 to 2.7 million km (1.2 to 1.7 million miles).

**At this distance Earth subtends roughly 0.23–0.17°** — similar to how the Moon appears from Earth's surface, but for the full disk. This is between DSCOVR's L1 vantage point and the Apollo lunar distance observations.

**Description:** Galileo's color composites (red, green, violet filters) show South America, Antarctica, and Pacific weather systems clearly. The images were used for a famous "life detection" test by Carl Sagan's team, who analyzed the spectrum and spatial patterns to confirm biosignatures. The atmosphere appears as a blue-tinged disk with the atmospheric limb present in the imaging data but, at this scale, indistinguishable from the overall planetary coloring. No airglow would be detectable at these distances in standard photography.

## 7. Cassini "Pale Blue Dot" (July 19, 2013)

**Mission:** Cassini orbiting Saturn. Distance to Earth: 1,445,858,000 km (approximately 9.65 AU, or 1.45 billion km).

**Appearance of Earth:** Earth and Moon appear as "mere dots — Earth a pale blue and the Moon a stark white" between Saturn's rings. Earth is a single bright pixel or small cluster of pixels in the raw images; color is the dominant (and essentially only) visible property.

**Atmospheric limb:** Not detectable at this scale. The entire planet is unresolved. The blue color of the dot results from the atmosphere's reflectance properties integrated across the whole disk — but the planet itself subtends a fraction of an arcsecond. No rim, no haze layer, no airglow. The pale blue color is the atmospheric signature, but it manifests as the dot's color, not as a rim feature.

## 8. DSCOVR / EPIC at L1 (1.5 million km)

**Mission:** NOAA DSCOVR (Deep Space Climate Observatory), launched February 2015, at the Sun-Earth L1 Lagrange point approximately 1.5 million km from Earth. Camera: EPIC (Earth Polychromatic Imaging Camera), 10-channel spectroradiometer (317–780 nm), 2048×2048 pixel CCD, 30 cm aperture Cassegrain telescope.

**Earth disk size from L1:** Earth subtends approximately 0.49° — about the same angular size as the Moon seen from Earth's surface, but showing the full illuminated disk.

### The blue limb in EPIC images

DSCOVR/EPIC images clearly show a blue atmospheric rim, and this has been characterized in multiple scientific papers:

- "The blue cast is rather ubiquitous over both land and ocean and gradually increases in brightness as we approach the limb." The limb of Earth in EPIC images appears softened — not sharply terminated — with a gradual brightening in the blue channel.
- The softening is caused by multiple effects: Rayleigh scattering (nitrogen and oxygen), ozone absorption, and aerosol scattering all contribute to the appearance of the limb zone.
- The limb is not a crisp edge; it is a diffuse blue brightening that transitions over several tens of kilometers of atmospheric path into the darkness of space.
- "Aerosols and ozone help to soften the appearance of the Earth near the limb in the DSCOVR images" — this makes the blue rim detectable and visually smooth rather than sharp.

**In terms of visual appearance:** From L1, the atmospheric limb appears as a thin but detectable blue-brightened rim around Earth's disk. It is not a bold colored band — it is a gentle diffuse brightening at the edge, especially in the blue channel. Scientists characterize it as a "bluish tint" that grows stronger toward the limb. Surface features (Amazon, Sahara, weather systems) are still clearly resolvable at 2048×2048 pixels.

### The "Gaia's Crown" effect

During a December 2015 lunar transit observed by DSCOVR, as the Moon slipped behind Earth's limb, EPIC captured what has been nicknamed "Gaia's Crown" — a thin, razor-bright flange of light where the Moon's edge was stretched and lifted by atmospheric refraction. This demonstrates that Earth's atmosphere is optically thick enough to produce significant refraction even at L1 distances, bending light around the limb in a detectable way.

### Airglow from L1

No airglow is detectable in DSCOVR/EPIC imagery. The EPIC instrument observes reflected solar radiation in narrowband channels from 317–780 nm. Night-side Earth is not imaged (EPIC always views the sunlit hemisphere). Airglow would require observing the darkened limb in specific emission wavelengths with far greater sensitivity than EPIC provides. The published EPIC papers make no mention of airglow detection.

## Summary: What Changes With Distance

### Blue atmospheric haze (daytime limb)

| Distance regime | Appearance |
|---|---|
| ISS (400 km) | Prominent stratified colored bands — orange troposphere, milky stratosphere, blue upper atmosphere. Visually striking. |
| 1,000–5,000 km | Still a prominent blue rim. The atmosphere appears as a vivid halo around the partly-visible Earth. |
| Geostationary (36,000 km) | Present but subtle. A diffuse blue-gray softening at the edge of the disk. Not a bold colored band. |
| Apollo / ~45,000 km | Thin but photographically distinct blue-white haze rim. Described as a "tiny sliver." |
| Lunar (~384,000 km) | Merged with the planet's edge. The atmosphere is physically there but does not resolve as a separate layer on film. Earth looks sharp-edged with the blue limb below film/sensor resolution. |
| L1 / 1.5 million km | Detectable as a diffuse blue brightening at the limb in high-resolution digital imagery. Present as a subtle softening effect, not a bold rim. Requires good sensor and image analysis. |
| Saturn (1.45 billion km) | Completely unresolved. Earth is a blue dot; the blue color itself is the atmospheric signature. |

### Airglow / nightglow

| Distance regime | Visibility |
|---|---|
| ISS (400 km) | Easily photographed in long exposures. Green band at ~90–100 km is a prominent, visually spectacular arc. Sometimes red band above it. |
| 1,000–5,000 km | Still photographically detectable at the limb using long exposures. Would appear as a thinner arc. Gemini 11 specifically flew an "Airglow Horizon Photography" experiment confirming this. |
| Geostationary (36,000 km) | Not detectable with any standard or weather satellite camera. Only detectable using specialized far-UV instrumentation (e.g., NASA GOLD mission at 132–162 nm). Invisible to the naked eye. |
| Lunar distance and beyond | Completely undetectable by any currently flown camera. The physics are conclusive: the surface brightness is far too low when integrated over the tiny solid angle subtended by the airglow arc. |

### Key physical principle for airglow visibility

Airglow is viewable edge-on from low orbit because the observer is looking through a long column of atmosphere (thousands of km of path length) which integrates the thin (~30 km thick) emission layer into a bright apparent band. As distance increases, two effects work against detectability: (1) the solid angle of the emission layer shrinks as the inverse square, reducing the received flux per pixel; (2) the geometric path-length advantage of edge-on viewing diminishes because even "edge-on" from far distance is looking through less integrated column than from nearby. The ISS is close enough that the airglow arc subtends a resolvable angle with practical exposure times. From GEO, the arc subtends roughly 90x less angle and receives 90x² less flux — making it utterly beyond the reach of any visible-light camera.

## Implications for the Sunlit Earth Renderer

1. **Blue atmospheric rim (daytime):** A correctly rendered atmospheric limb should show a diffuse blue brightening at the edge of the disk. It should NOT be a sharp edge — it should soften gradually. At the viewing distances Sunlit Earth uses (where the full disk is visible), this corresponds to the GEO-through-lunar distance regime: a thin but visible blue-gray softening at the limb. The DSCOVR/EPIC description "gradual increase in blue brightness approaching the limb" with a "softened, diffuse" appearance is the target.

2. **Airglow:** At viewing distances where the full Earth disk is visible (equivalent to GEO or beyond), physically accurate rendering would show **no visible airglow** — it would be below the threshold of visibility. Airglow as a prominent visual feature is an ISS-altitude phenomenon. Including a stylized green airglow arc at the limb in a rendering viewed from "geostationary to lunar" distances is physically unrealistic, though it might be included for artistic purposes or as a configurable feature.

3. **Atmospheric depth:** From the user's typical viewing distance, the atmosphere occupies roughly 1.5–2% of the Earth's radius as a visible rim. The blue rim should be visually thin but present — consistent with the "tiny sliver" description from Apollo 17 at ~45,000 km.

4. **Limb darkening vs. limb brightening:** In reflected sunlight, Earth's limb actually shows a subtle brightening in the blue channel (Rayleigh scattering is enhanced at grazing angles), which is the opposite of simple geometric limb darkening. Correctly modeled, the very edge of Earth should appear slightly bluer/brighter than the center in clear-sky regions — which is what DSCOVR scientists observe and model.

## Reference Missions (for Image Searching)

- **Apollo 8 Earthrise** (1968, Bill Anders, Hasselblad) — lunar distance, sharp disk, no visible airglow
- **Apollo 17 Blue Marble** (1972, Harrison Schmitt or Ronald Evans, Hasselblad) — ~29,000–45,000 km, thin blue sliver atmosphere visible
- **Gemini 11 airglow horizon photos** (1966, Richard Gordon, 1,374 km altitude) — closest regime to ISS that attempted systematic airglow photography
- **Polaris Dawn Earth images** (2024, SpaceX Crew Dragon, ~1,400 km) — recent high-resolution digital imagery from comparable altitude
- **MESSENGER Earth flyby movie** (2005, MDIS camera, 2,347 km to 435,885 km) — crescent and full-disk views at multiple distances
- **Galileo Earth spin movie** (1990, SSI camera, ~2–2.7 million km) — deep space full-disk imaging
- **DSCOVR/EPIC daily imagery** (2015–present, EPIC camera, 1.5 million km) — the canonical deep-space full-disk Earth image; blue atmospheric limb detectable
- **NASA GOLD mission** (2018–present, UV spectrograph from GEO) — airglow from GEO, but far-UV only, not visible-light
- **Himawari-8 AHI lunar transit videos** (JMA, 36,000 km) — atmospheric refraction at GEO during lunar occultations ("Gaia's Crown" analog)
- **ISS atmospheric limb photography** (ongoing, many missions; search NASA EOL database for "atmospheric limb") — the baseline reference for what airglow actually looks like
