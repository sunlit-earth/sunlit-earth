# Plan Amendment: The Sun at the Horizon

Amends `2026-08-23-celestial-b-sun-plan.md`. Written 2026-08-29, after the finding its Review Rounds section records and `docs/roadmap.md` carries as "Bodies at the limb": the composite does not read well while the Sun's disk is partly behind the globe or barely clearing it. Phases C and D have landed, which is what that item was waiting for. This amendment settles what happens at the limb, adds the controls the review asked for, and grounds each effect in what the Sun does when it crosses the Earth's horizon seen from space.

The user's review of 2026-08-29 describes the failure and the target. Today, as the Sun nears the painted horizon the glare all but vanishes and the Sun reads as a huge but very distant star; once the disk is partly occluded it is a clipped white shape with no glare at all. What it should do: a disk barely over the horizon is orange to red and already carries a visible glare; the glare is uniform in every direction, and the one thing that is not uniform is the atmosphere, which takes an orange-red band around the Sun wider than the glare, since that is where the light is scattered; the more of the disk clears the horizon, the brighter and whiter it gets; the Sun may be brightest at the moment it stands fully clear, as an eye that has not yet adjusted would see it, and settles to its ordinary brightness as it climbs. Everything below is either that, or the realism that has to sit under it, and every effect is a control so the trade between the two is the user's.

## What changes, and what does not

Unchanged: the two-draw structure (disk with the sky, glare last), the Spencer composition in angular coordinates, the stereographic sky lens, occlusion measured against the painted silhouette rather than the true limb (decision 2), the pixel floor, camera mode, the refusal to build an HDR pipeline (decision 7), and every test the plan's departures record except the grazing golden, which exists to move.

Changed:

## Research

Recorded 2026-08-29. Web sources were read by subagents and the quotations below are theirs; the three papers were extracted with `pdftotext` and read directly; the derived figures are computed in this section and their inputs named. ### What the light does

From orbit the Sun sets and rises through the atmosphere twice over: the ray from the Sun enters the atmosphere, grazes its lowest point, and leaves again on the way to the observer. Two things happen along that path and they are the whole of the physics here.

**Refraction.** Mignard (Comptes Rendus Physique 23, 2022, on Don Pettit's ISS moonset photographs of 2003-04-16) computes for an observer at 400 km: the horizon is 2300 km away at a depression close to 20 degrees; "La réfraction du rayon rasant vaut 65,5', soit exactement le double de la réfraction du rayon horizontal sur Terre au niveau de la mer" (the grazing ray's refraction is 65.5 arcminutes, exactly twice the sea-level horizontal refraction); the rate at which refraction changes with apparent altitude "est 25 fois plus élevé à bord de la station" (is 25 times higher aboard the station), so that where on the ground 90 percent of the maximum refraction accrues over the last 15 degrees above the horizon, from the station it accrues over 0.75 degrees; the vertical magnification at the horizon is "< 0,2 soit une dimension verticale de la lune n'atteignant pas 0,1" (below 0.2, the Moon's vertical dimension not reaching a tenth of a degree); and the sequence took "une dizaine de secondes" (about ten seconds). Mallama (lunar eclipse model, 2022) has the same doubling with the same reason: "Since a ray of light that just grazes sea level on its way to an eclipsed Moon travels twice as far through the atmosphere as the setting Sun ray, it is refracted by 70'", and "refraction is practically independent of color", so the reddening below is extinction and not dispersion. Kipping (2019) puts the surface-grazing deflection at "just over one degree". Atmospheric Optics describes the shape sequence Pettit photographed: "At first the lower limb is distorted most", the Moon becomes "an impossibly flattened oval", and "the lower part of the disk and then the entire moon is appreciably reddened by preferential atmospheric scattering of blue light out of the ray path". No photograph of the Sun itself flattened from orbit was found; the mechanism is stated for the Sun and the published example is the Moon.

**Extinction.** Mallama's Table 3.2 gives the transmission of green light for a ray whose lowest point is at a given altitude, with the cumulative air mass along the way, against 73 percent per air mass for blue light and 90 percent for red ("Typical values of transmission range from about 73% for a vertical ray of blue light up to 90% for red"). The layout extraction interleaved the table's altitude column, so the pairing below was reconstructed by matching from the bottom row up and checked against an exponential atmosphere: m = 70 e^(-h / 7 km) reproduces every air mass in the table to within about a fifth, and all but one of the rows below 20 km to within a tenth, and Kipping's ray tracing fits the same exponential to refraction with "an effective scale height for the lensing, equal to 6.911 km". The subagent that transcribed the table paired it the other way, reading each altitude printed on the same line as its row, which gives 50 percent transmission at 7.9 km and 40 percent at 2.6 km; that is rejected here because a ray skimming 2.6 km above the sea crosses some fifty air masses by the paper's own density argument, and because it leaves seven rows with no altitude where the source prints thirteen altitudes for the thirteen rows below the top one. Kipping's lowtran7 extinction for a sea-level path, "B-band was found to be 0.45, V-band 0.28, R-band 0.19" magnitudes per air mass, is a second source for the chromatic ratio and is consistent with Mallama's 73 and 90 percent.

The hue column is (0.90^m, 0.836^m, 0.73^m) scaled so the largest channel is one, with 0.836 for green fitted to the table's own pairs. Two things follow. The Sun's disk stays saturated in an 8-bit image down to a lowest altitude of about 9 km, because a tenth of the Sun is still a blinding source, and what changes over that range is its hue: white above 30 km, yellow around 20, deep orange at 13, red below 10. And below about 5 km the ray carries a thousandth or less, which is why the setting disk fades out above the hard horizon rather than being cut by it; Kipping adds that any cloud along the grazing path, "even wispy high-altitude cirrus clouds", is opaque over a path of up to 100 km, so the real bottom of the band is the cloud tops. Pettit's own summary is the one line that says all of it: "In space, brilliant red-hued sunrise happens in 7 1/2 seconds instead of 2 minutes as on Earth. Orbital views shift toward red due to the longer distance the light rays travel through the atmosphere."

**Refraction and extinction together (derived).** With the ISS horizon at 2300 km, one arcminute of apparent altitude is 0.67 km of the ray's lowest altitude, so the 65.5 arcminute lift at the horizon equals 44 km of it: a Sun whose rays graze the surface is seen nearly halfway up the visible band, not on the hard horizon, and by the time the image reaches the hard horizon the true Sun is 65 arcminutes below it, more than two disk diameters. The Sun's 32 arcminute disk spans 21 km of lowest altitude, so its bottom is deep red while its top is yellow. Taking refraction proportional to density with the 7 km scale height (Kipping's 6.911 km for refraction, and the value that fits Mallama's air masses), the apparent-to-true elevation map is e_true = e_app - 65.5' e^(-h / 7 km), whose slope at the horizon is 1 + (65.5 / 7) x 0.67 = 7.3, a vertical magnification of 0.14 at the horizon, 0.33 at 8 km, 0.61 at 16 km, 0.83 at 24 km and 0.94 at 32 km. That agrees with Mignard's "< 0,2" to the accuracy an exponential deserves, and it is what the squash in decision 11 uses; one scale height serves the air mass and the refraction alike.

**Timing.** The station's angular rate is 360 degrees in 93 minutes, 0.0645 degrees per second, so the disk crosses a geometric horizon in about eight seconds; refraction holds the lower limb and stretches that, which is Pettit's seven and a half seconds for the color and Mignard's ten for the Moon. NASA's captions say "each sunrise or sunset only lasts a few seconds". Astronaut Joseph Allen: "The sun truly 'comes up like thunder,' and it sets just as fast. Each sunrise and sunset lasts only a few seconds. But in that time you see at least eight different bands of color come and go, from a brilliant red to the brightest and deepest blue."

### What photographs show

The Earth Observatory captions are consistent about the band's vertical structure at sunrise and sunset from the ISS. Sunset over the Indian Ocean, 2010-05-25, Nikon D3, 400 mm: "Deep oranges and yellows appear in the troposphere, which extends from the Earth's surface to 6–20 km high"; "The pink to white region above the clouds appears to be the lower stratosphere"; "Above the stratosphere, blue layers likely mark the transition between the middle and upper atmosphere as it gradually fades into the blackness of outer space"; "Variations in the colors are due mainly to varying concentrations of either clouds or aerosols". Sunrise over the southern Indian Ocean, 2023-09-29, Nikon D5, 170 mm: "The lowest layer, known as the troposphere, appears orange and red as these wavelengths of light are scattered by particles of dust, smoke, and smog. Directly above the troposphere is the stratosphere. This blue layer is usually cloud-free". EarthSky's reading of an ISS limb frame adds the brown tropopause between the orange troposphere and the milky stratosphere. The troposphere is "roughly 15 kilometers (10 miles) thick", which from the ISS is 0.4 degrees at the horizon; the whole band to 100 km is 2.7 degrees there (derived from the depression angles at 400 km; the 0.85 degree figure in `2026-03-22-atmosphere-visual-reference.md` divides by the distance to the Earth's center rather than to the horizon and is low by a factor of three).

What the Sun itself looks like in those frames is mostly not stated. The one caption that describes it, sunrise over the Philippine Sea, 2017-07-26, Nikon D4, 500 mm: the astronaut captured "the partial disc of the Sun just as it began to rise, creating a sheet of light across the horizon", with "Numerous individual layers of the atmosphere appear above the Sun from this perspective". So the first impression of a rising Sun is a horizon-long sheet of light with a partial disk in it, which is the forward-scattering lobe of decision 12 rather than a compact glow. Pettit, listing what a careful eye sees sixteen times a day, names "forward reddened lobes, off-axis blue lobes, and corona halos" (indexed from his Smithsonian Air & Space essay, which returned 403; wording unverified at source). No source describes the glare's symmetry, its size relative to the band, or lens ghosts in words, and none quantifies how far along the limb the orange region extends relative to the Sun's azimuth. The one indirect statement is that the Sun's glare hides everything near it in a daylight frame: the planets in Karen Nyberg's sunrise photograph appeared only after processing, because "the glare of the Sun's light hid them in her photo".

The far vantage, which is the renderer's own, has two photographs and both are of a Sun geometrically behind the Earth. Apollo 12, 1969-11-21: "only a sliver of a crescent is visible, with a single point glowing large and bright like the jewel on a ring". Kaguya, 2009-02-10, from lunar orbit: "since the view of the Sun from the KAGUYA was mostly covered by the Earth, the KAGUYA observed that the Earth looked like a diamond ring". That is refracted light with no disk at all reading as one overwhelming point on the limb, and it is what true geometry would give this renderer at every zoom it offers; see decision 11 for why the composite does not draw it.

### What glare does, and what an observer does

The plan's Spencer model is an observer's point spread function; the amendment needs how its amplitude scales with the source, and the standard statement is the Stiles-Holladay disability glare formula, L_v = 10 E / theta^2, "veiling luminance (Lv), illumination in lux (E) incident on the cornea, and the angle in degrees (theta) between the line of sight and glare source" (CIE 146:2002 extends it and keeps the form). Veiling glare is proportional to the illuminance the source delivers at the eye, which is its flux: visible area times transmission. That is the physical half. The perceptual half is Stevens' power law, with brightness exponents of 0.33 for a "5° target in dark" and 0.5 for a "point source": perceived brightness is a compressive function of flux, which is why a tenth of the Sun does not look a tenth as bright.

The exposure half is where the user's cheat lives, and it is a real thing on a real timescale. Light adaptation is fast but not instant: the pupil's constriction "takes approximately 2 sec for a small pupilloconstriction and approximately 5 sec for maximal constriction", cones then keep adjusting "in a time scale of tens of seconds", and industry references put the whole of light adaptation at "within seconds" against dark adaptation's thirty minutes. A camera's automatic exposure settles in a fraction of a second to a couple of seconds (only a high-speed-camera vendor's figure was found; no camera-agnostic consensus). An orbital sunrise lasts seven to ten seconds. So neither an eye nor a camera exposed for the night side is adapted when the disk clears the band, and both are by the time the Sun has climbed a few degrees: the flux is what it is, and what the picture shows is flux times an exposure that lags. A still frame has no time axis, so decision 10 maps the lag onto altitude above the band, which at the station's rate is the same thing: one minute of adaptation is four degrees of rise, seven disk diameters.

The primary Spencer paper could not be fetched this session (mirrors refused), so the statement that its PSF's energy scales with source flux while its shape does not depend on source size is inferred from the method rather than quoted; the Stiles-Holladay form carries the same content and is quoted.

### What the composite does today, and why that is the failure

`fs_sun_disk` writes white at every fragment. `fs_sun_glare` multiplies the whole composition by `sun_glow * sun_visible` and, while any of the disk is in the transit annulus, mixes its tint toward one fixed orange and multiplies by `mix(1.0, 0.6, sun_transit)`. So a disk that is 97 percent visible and 57 percent in the band, which is the grazing golden, is a white disk with a glare at 0.58 of clear-sky strength, and a disk a tenth visible is a white sliver with a glare at 0.06: the huge distant star, then the glareless white shape. The visible fraction is the right physical input and the wrong perceptual output, for the two reasons above: nothing compresses it, and nothing models the exposure.

The geometry the composite works in has to be kept in view when reading the physics against it. The renderer's cameras sit 4 to 80 Earth radii out; from 9 radii (zoom 0.45) the whole band from the surface to `RAYLEIGH_RADIUS` is 0.11 degrees, a fifth of the disk, and from 80 radii a fiftieth. True refraction there would be a lift of over a degree against a band a tenth of a degree thick and a vertical magnification near a hundredth: the disk would be a line on the hard horizon while the ephemeris had the Sun two diameters behind the Earth, which is the Apollo 12 jewel. The plan's decision 2 already chose to be right about what the viewer sees rather than about where the Sun is, because the mixed lens paints the globe about four and a half times larger than the sky lens images its silhouette. This amendment applies the same rule to the band: the painted annulus between the globe's silhouette and the atmosphere shell's is the visible atmosphere, its full width stands for the 96 km from the surface to the shell, and every effect below is expressed in that annulus's own units with the ISS-derived dimensionless coefficients. On the reference framings that annulus is 1.2 pixels wide at zoom 0.45 on a 512 by 256 frame and 2.3 at zoom 0.30, against a disk 4.6 pixels across at 60 degrees of sky; on a 4K wallpaper the same two zooms give 10 and 19 pixels against a disk 34 across. So at wallpaper resolution the band holds a third to a half of the disk, the gradient across the disk is visible, and at preview sizes it is a pixel, which is what decision 9's `sun_horizon_depth` exists for.

## Decisions (continuing the plan's numbering)

8. **Three controls the review asked for: `sun_size`, `sun_halo_radius`, and the flare cap.** `sun_size` (1 to 8, default 1) multiplies the disk's angular radius before the pixel floor, exactly as `moon_size` multiplies the Moon's, and the visibility arithmetic measures the disk that is drawn, so a Moon crossing an enlarged Sun hides the fraction of it the picture shows. What follows the disk and what does not is decided by what a glare is: the PSF is convolved with the source's image, so a larger disk has a flat-topped bloom out to its own edge and the same falloff beyond it. The inner bloom lobe therefore keeps its thickness around the disk, `SUN_BLOOM_INNER_DEGREES + (size - 1) * SUN_ANGULAR_RADIUS_DEGREES`, and the corona's inner cutoff follows because it already reads `sun_disk_radius`; the outer lobe, the needles' reach and the halo ring stay in absolute degrees, because they are properties of the eye and not of the source. `sun_halo_radius` (1.5 to 8 degrees, default 3) is the lenticular ring's angular radius, with its width scaling in proportion (the 1.1 to 3.0 ratio the shader has). The review's phrase was "the radius of the glare ring"; the ring is the one thing in the composition that is a ring, and the user confirmed that reading on 2026-08-29. `sun_flare` is capped at 1.0 with a default of 0.15, and `AppConfig::sanitize` clamps it, the `camera_fov` precedent: a file holding 1.8 would otherwise draw a flare the slider cannot show. `sun_rays` already defaults to 0.6 and displays as 60 percent; confirmed, not changed.

9. **The disk and the glare are colored by the light path, per fragment, from the painted annulus.** The uniform buffer gains the globe's screen circle and the atmosphere shell's radius (four floats, the same circles `place_sun` already computes), and one WGSL function `limb_transmission(position)` mirrored by one Rust function of the same name does the rest: the fragment's distance outside the globe circle divided by the horizon zone's width is its height in the band, the band's width stands for 96 km, the air mass is `70 * exp(-h / 7 km) * sun_reddening`, and the transmission per channel is `(0.90, 0.836, 0.73)^m`, Mallama's values with green fitted to his table. `fs_sun_disk` multiplies its white by that color normalized to its largest channel, and by `saturate(T_green * 40)^0.5`, so the disk clips at full brightness wherever the ray carries more than 2.5 percent (about 9 km) and fades out below, which is the band's lowest few kilometers, instead of being cut where the painted globe begins. The function takes a framebuffer position and nothing about the Sun, so the Moon's follow-up can call it from `fs_moon` unchanged. The glare's color is the mean of that normalized color over the visible disk, computed on the CPU by a strip quadrature across the disk's radial extent and handed to the shader as one vec3; the flat orange `SUN_TRANSIT_COLOR` and the 0.6 dimming go, and `sun_transit` leaves the uniform buffer, though `SunVisibility::transit_fraction` stays for the tests that read it. `sun_reddening` (0 to 2, default 1) scales the air mass: 0 is the white Sun of today, 1 is the table, 2 is twice the atmosphere.

   The horizon zone's width is `max(painted annulus, sun_horizon_depth * disk diameter)` in pixels. At 0 the zone is the geometric annulus and nothing else, which is the physical reading and is one to two pixels on a preview at the default zooms; the default of 1 disk diameter is what makes the gradient across the disk exist on a preview at all, and it is also about what the ISS sees, where the band is four and a half disks and the disk's own span of it is a fifth. `sun_horizon_depth` runs 0 to 4. This one number is the unit every other horizon effect is measured in.

10. **The glare's amplitude is the visible flux through a compressive response and a lagging exposure, and the lag is the user's cheat made into a slider.** On the CPU, `place_sun` computes the flux F as the fraction of the disk's area outside the globe circle weighted by `T_green` at each strip's height, so a disk half behind the limb with its visible half in the red carries far less than half the flux, and a disk clear of the band carries 1. The glare draws `sun_glow * X * F^0.5`: the exponent is Stevens' point-source value and is the LDR stand-in for the compression the plan's decision 7 declined to build an HDR pipeline for; F^0.5 at a tenth of the disk is 0.32, so a sliver glares at once. X is the exposure gain: `sun_horizon_boost` while the disk's lower edge is at or below the top of the horizon zone, decaying smoothly to 1 over `sun_horizon_reach` zone widths above it. That makes the glare climb from first light, peak at the moment the whole disk stands clear of the band, and settle as it climbs, which is the sequence the review described, and it is the adaptation lag with altitude standing in for time. `sun_horizon_boost` runs 1 to 8, default 3; at 1 the model is the physical one and there is no peak. `sun_horizon_reach` runs 0.5 to 10, default 4; a minute of adaptation at the station's rate is seven disk diameters and the pupil's two seconds are a quarter of one, so 4 is between the two and is a taste default for the screenshot loop to settle. The disk itself takes no gain: it is clipped already.

11. **Refraction is a lift and a squash in the horizon zone's own units, behind one slider.** Both are functions of the disk center's geometric height in the zone, u, in zone widths, negative behind the painted limb. The lift is `0.46 * exp(-13.7 u)` zone widths, radially away from the globe's center: 65.5 arcminutes is 44 km of ray height at the ISS horizon, 0.46 of the 96 km band, and 96 over 7 is the exponent. The apparent height solves `u_true = u_app - 0.46 exp(-13.7 u_app)` for `u_app` (a few Newton steps; the map is monotonic for u_app >= 0), and the vertical magnification is its slope's inverse, `1 / (1 + 6.3 exp(-13.7 u_app))`: 0.14 at the horizon, 0.94 at a third of the zone, the numbers derived in the research. The apparent center is written back into `sun_view_dir` by rotating the true direction away from the globe's center by the lift converted through the pixels-per-degree at the Sun, so the disk, the glare and every angular falloff move together with no second spelling of the position; the squash goes to the shader as one float and `fs_sun_disk` measures an elliptical distance, radial component divided by the squash, with the quad's extent left at the unsquashed radius. Visibility of a squashed disk against the limb is the unsquashed circle's visibility against a limb moved by the same factor, because scaling along the cut's normal preserves area fractions, so `visibility` needs no ellipse arithmetic; at `sun_refraction` 0 every function returns exactly what it returns today, which is what keeps the existing unit tests and both sun engine cases as they are. The slider runs 0 to 2, default 1: 0 is the geometric Sun, 1 the derived coefficients, 2 twice them. What it buys at the default zoom is small, since the zone is a disk diameter and the lift half of one; at close zooms and on a wallpaper it is the flattened red disk clinging to the horizon that is the signature of an orbital sunset, and first light half a disk before the geometric rise. Not taken: true-geometry refraction, which at 9 Earth radii would put the Sun's image on the hard horizon as a line while the ephemeris has it two diameters behind the Earth. That is the honest far-vantage picture, the Apollo 12 jewel, and it is recorded in Alternatives as the thing to draw if the composite ever goes single-lens.

12. **The Rayleigh shell carries a forward-scattering lobe with a real phase function, which is the boost phase B tried and dropped done with the view ray instead of the surface normal.** Departure 3 of the plan measured the `n_dot_l` version at 0.007 of 255 and the research document's section 5.4 says why: `n_dot_l` is what the existing terms already weight the ring by, and a phase function wants the view ray. `fs_rayleigh` has the view ray. The scattering angle is `acos(-dot(sun_dir, v))`, forward where the Sun is behind the fragment as seen from the eye, which is exactly the limb under a rising Sun; the lobe is Henyey-Greenstein in that angle, `atmo_sunrise_width` (10 to 90 degrees, default 30) setting the angle at which it falls to half through the asymmetry parameter; its color is the hue `limb_transmission` leaves at the fragment's own ray height, `h = (r * sqrt(1 - n_dot_v^2) - 1) * 6371 km` on the shell of radius r, that transmission over its largest channel the way the disk's own color is, so it is red at the bottom of the rim and white at the top, over the blue the existing term already scatters; it is gated by the fragment being sunlit (`smoothstep(-0.2, 0.0, n_dot_l)`, since the atmosphere at 96 km sees the Sun some ten degrees past the surface terminator) and scaled by `atmo_sunrise_glow` (0 to 3, default 1) times the existing `rim` and `rayleigh_intensity`. Both lenses are radially symmetric about the view axis and take the same pan, so the lobe's azimuth around the limb is the Sun's image's azimuth, while its width along the limb is in true angle and is a taste parameter for that reason. Both controls sit in the Atmosphere group, because the effect is the atmosphere's and is drawn only while `atmo_enabled`; the Sun group's hints name them. The shader cost is one dot, one `pow(x, 1.5)` and one exponential per rim fragment, next to the exponential the rim already pays.

13. **Twelve controls, full digest treatment, and the goldens pin the old defaults.** The new fields go through `AppConfig`, `SceneParams`, the digest and its table test, the uniforms, the Slint bridge and `slint_ui.rs`, as every parameter does. `base_params()` in `tests/golden.rs` and `test_params()` in `tests/engine.rs` set `sun_flare: 0.0` explicitly, because five references contain the Sun (`sun_over_the_night_side`, the three star cases at their shared night-side camera, and `nightglow`'s bloom tail) and a default of 0.15 would put spikes and ghosts in all of them and make the glare quad fullscreen. The horizon defaults are left at their defaults in both, which is what lets the criteria below say which references may move.

## Risks

- Taste, again, and more of it: five coupled horizon controls. Step 3 is the dedicated loop, every knob is a slider, and the cold-realism setting is one row of the Controls table, so a bad default is recoverable by the user and the physical answer is always reachable.
- The horizon zone is a pixel or two on a preview at the default zooms. `sun_horizon_depth` at 1 disk diameter is what keeps the effects visible there; at 0 they collapse into the annulus and a user who asks for that gets the physically thin band. The goldens are framed at a close zoom for the same reason phase B's ran at 60 degrees of sky.
- The flare default moves goldens unless pinned. Decision 13 pins it; criterion 3 checks the pin by regeneration on both adapters.
- The refraction lift makes a Sun visible before its geometric rise, so a framing that today shows nothing could show a red line. Both engine cases that assert an empty frame have the Sun well inside the painted disc, where the lift (at most 0.46 zone widths) cannot reach; verify rather than assume, since the zone width is the disk diameter at the default depth.
- The lobe's width is in true angle on a shell painted through the Earth lens; its azimuth is right by symmetry, its extent is taste. If a screenshot shows the lobe reaching where the glare does not, that is the effect the review asked for; if it reads as a painted stripe, narrow the default.
- Cross-adapter stability of `pow` in the transmission and the Henyey-Greenstein lobe: both are smooth functions of smooth inputs, unlike the needle hash, and the existing tolerance should hold; if it does not, quantize the height into the band before the exponential rather than loosening the tolerance.
- Until the Moon's follow-up lands, a Moon and a Sun setting together take the limb differently: the Sun reddens, fades and flattens, the Moon is cut white at the painted limb as it is today. That is the deferral the user chose, recorded rather than hidden.

## Questions, settled

Four questions were open when this amendment was drafted; the user answered all four on 2026-08-29.

1. "The radius of the glare ring" means the lenticular halo. `sun_halo_radius` stands as decision 8 has it; the alternative reading, one multiplier on every angular scale of the glare, is not taken.
2. `sun_horizon_reach` and `sun_horizon_depth` stay separate controls.
3. The Moon is handled in a follow-up of its own, tint and squash alike. `limb_transmission` is written to be callable from `fs_moon` and nothing here calls it there. What that follow-up will weigh: the squash is a non-uniform scale on the model matrix toward the globe's center plus a lift, both CPU-side and cheap, but the Moon's own crossing is a hard cut by draw order, so a squashed Moon would still be cut at the painted limb, and the tint's fade in the lowest kilometers is what would soften that.
4. Every default in the Controls table is provisional and is refined by the user once the implementation can be looked at; the screenshot loops of steps 3 and 5 produce the candidates and Departures records what shipped.

## Alternatives considered

- **An HDR intermediate with convolution bloom.** Rejected again for the plan's decision 7 reasons; F^0.5 and the exposure gain are the LDR stand-ins and cost two multiplies.
- **True-geometry refraction.** At the renderer's distances it draws the Apollo 12 jewel: a squashed line on the hard horizon while the Sun is geometrically hidden, and a band a tenth of a degree thick. Coherent with a single-lens composite and incoherent with this one, for the reason decision 2 already gave; recorded as what to draw if the mixed lens ever goes.
- **Keying the tint on the transit fraction, as the plan did.** One scalar cannot give a disk a gradient, and the gradient is what the photographs show: red at the bottom, yellow at the top, 21 km of ray height across one disk from the ISS.
- **A textured sunrise sprite.** The plan's section 5.2 already declined shipped textures for the Sun; nothing here changes that.
- **The `n_dot_l` forward-scatter boost.** Tried in phase B, 0.007 of 255, dropped; decision 12 is its replacement and names why the first attempt could not have worked.

## Departures and validation record

Recorded during implementation on 2026-08-29, in the order the amendment met reality.

1. **The uniform block carries `sun_size` rather than a precomputed bloom scale, and gained a flux of its own.** Step 1's list names "the bloom inner scale" as a uniform. Passing the size instead keeps `SUN_BLOOM_INNER_DEGREES` in the shader where its five siblings are; computing the scale on the CPU would have put a shader constant on the Rust side, which is the duplication the shared-rule probe exists to police. `sun_flux` is beyond the list for a different reason: `sun_visible` is still what culls the glare draw, the flux is what scales it, and after decision 10 those are two different numbers.

2. **The refracted direction is recovered through the lens rather than through a pixels-per-degree linearization.** Decision 11 says the apparent center is written back "by rotating the true direction away from the globe's center by the lift converted through the pixels-per-degree at the Sun". `place_sun` instead moves the disc's center radially by the lift in pixels and inverts the sky lens analytically (`sky_lens_direction`, the Rust twin of the shader's function of the same name), then re-images the disc from the direction that comes back. Same intent, exact rather than linearized, and it reuses one formula instead of adding a second. A lift of exactly zero skips the round trip, so a Sun the band cannot reach keeps its bits.

3. **The squashed-visibility identity is exact for a straight limb and approximate for a circular one.** Decision 11 says scaling along the cut's normal preserves area fractions, which holds where the limb is a line. Scaling one axis takes the globe's circle to an ellipse, so the pulled-back limb agrees with the moved circle where the disk sits and curves away to either side of it. Measured at the strongest flattening the slider reaches, against the drawn ellipse sampled on a 400 by 400 grid: 0.025 of the visible fraction at the true half degree, 0.065 at three times it, 0.121 at eight. It is worst where the disk is deepest in the band, which is where its light is nearly gone, so a glare already down to a hundredth is what carries the error. Kept rather than replaced, because the closed form is what returns exactly 1.0 for a clear disk, and every golden with a Sun above the limb depends on that.

4. **The forward lobe has its backscatter floor subtracted and the remainder renormalized.** Decision 12 says the lobe is Henyey-Greenstein in the scattering angle. Plain Henyey-Greenstein keeps 3.3 percent of its peak at every backscattering angle at the default width, which is a warm ring around the whole limb of every frame whose Sun is behind the camera: not a lobe around anything. The floor is subtracted and the rest renormalized, which is what `star_halo_profile` does at its own edge. With it, `default` and `rayleigh` moved by 0.0002 and 0.0087 of a level; without it both move by several.

5. **Criterion 1's twenty percent on the transmitted green does not hold below fifteen kilometers, and cannot.** The exponential air mass reproduces Mallama's table to within 21 percent at every row of it, but transmission is exponential in air mass, so a sixth of an air mass at eleven air masses is 41 percent of the green. Ratios of model to table: 0.98 at 32 km, 0.97 at 27, 0.94 at 22, 0.95 at 20, 0.94 at 18, 1.04 at 15, then 1.41 at 13, 1.31 at 8 and 1.66 at 5. `the_transmitted_green_follows_the_table_where_the_air_mass_is_small` holds the top six rows to twenty percent and pins the 13 km row at 1.41 with the reason beside it.

6. **Criterion 3's prediction about which references come back byte for byte is wrong for two of them.** The criterion named `sun_grazing_the_limb` as the one that moves and predicted that `sun_over_the_night_side`, the three star cases, `nightglow` and every reference without a Sun would be unchanged. Regenerated on both local adapters, five moved rather than one, and the two extra are the forward lobe rather than a regression: `nightglow` is a night-side framing whose Sun is behind the globe, which is exactly where the lobe fires, and it reddens 390 pixels of its limb past the outlier threshold. `default` and `rayleigh` are day-side frames where the lobe is at its subtracted floor, and what is left of it there is one or two levels. The whole measurement is in the validation record below. Byte for byte on both adapters, as predicted: `close_up`, `night_side_with_stars`, `large_crisp_stars`, `bright_star_halos`, `moon_crescent`, `panorama_behind_the_stars` and `panorama_at_a_narrow_sky`.

7. **The two new goldens are framed at zoom 0.26 rather than the 0.10 criterion 3 named.** The criterion asked for "a zoom where the zone holds the disk (about 0.10, where the painted annulus is 7 pixels on 512 by 256)". They run at 0.26, which is `base_params`' own zoom and the one every other case in the suite is framed at, where the annulus is 2.7 pixels and the zone is `sun_horizon_depth` disk diameters instead, which at the default depth is exactly the disk. What that costs is a narrower zone than the criterion had in mind, since the annulus it names is wider than the disk and the zone it names is therefore the annulus. Departure 9 is where that bill arrives and what pays it is the Sun's own size rather than the zoom, because the zoom is what puts these two cases against the same globe the other fourteen sit against.

8. **`sunrise_band` compares the strip of frame the band runs down, at three times the shipped band brightness.** The plan asked for two goldens and said nothing about windows. The band is a thread along a limb the grid texture already paints bright, so deleting the lobe entirely comes to a mean of 0.21 over the whole frame with 0.40 percent of pixels outliers, which passes on both counts: the reference would go on passing with the feature deleted. Over a 72 by 256 strip with the limb down the middle of it the same loss is 1.52 and 2.83 percent, which fails on the second. That is the treatment `moon_crescent` already needed, for the same arithmetic reason. The strip is not the whole of it: the case renders at `atmo_sunrise_glow` 3 rather than the shipped 1, the way `sun_grazing_the_limb` turns the glare up and `sun_rising_through_the_band` turns the Sun's size up. The lobe is scaled linearly by that control, so at the shipped default the same deletion over the same strip removes about a third as much light, and both numbers fall with it: the mean toward 0.5, and the outlier fraction faster still, since the 24 level threshold is fixed and fewer pixels clear it as the amplitude falls. Inside the tolerance on both counts is a case that cannot fail, which is why it is turned up. Every `sunrise_band` row of the teeth table below is measured at 3 and not at the shipped default.

9. **`sun_rising_through_the_band` needed the same treatment and one more: four times the Sun's size.** As first framed, at longitude 157.1 and the true half degree, the disk had barely entered the band: deleting the reddening moved the whole frame by a mean of 0.26 with no pixel differing by more than eight levels, and deleting the other two moved it less. The arithmetic says why. The disk is 4.6 pixels across there, the horizon zone is the same 4.6 pixels because the depth default makes it the disk's diameter, one zone width stands for the shell's 96 km, and the part of the band that colors anything is its lowest thirty kilometers, which is a pixel and a half. `sun_size` scales the disk and the zone together, so at four times the size the whole geometry is the shipped one at four times the pixels, which is what `moon_size` does for `moon_crescent` and for the same reason. The case is now at longitude 157.3 with `sun_size` 4, where the disk crosses the painted limb with 83 percent of itself showing, carries 36 percent of its light and is flattened to 0.57 of its height, and it compares the 80 by 80 window the disk and the near half of its glare land in. Measured there, deleting the tint comes to a mean of 55.33 with 83.09 percent of pixels outliers, the exposure gain to 56.03 with 98.58, and the lift and the squash to 6.19 with 4.58. The window is what the third one needs: over the whole frame at the same framing those are 10.04 with 16.80 percent, 7.27 with 11.00, and 0.91 with 0.22, so a full-frame reference would pass with refraction deleted.

10. **Criterion 4's "brightest pixel" cannot tell the exposure gain anything, so the engine cases measure the light the Sun adds.** The criterion asks that "the glare's brightest pixel at the moment the disk clears the zone exceeds its value with the Sun well clear, and equals it at boost 1". The disk is drawn clipped white wherever it is drawn at all, so any framing that shows any of it has a brightest gain of 254 over the same frame with `sun_glow` at zero, at every exposure and both boosts: the measurement is constant where the criterion needs it to move. What the gain multiplies is the glare's amplitude, so the cases sum what the Sun adds to the frame instead. At the two framings the criterion names, and with everything but the gain equal at both, the one at the top of the zone adds 2.74 times what the one well clear of it does, and 0.971 times with the boost at 1. The 2.74 rather than 3 and the 0.971 rather than 1 are both clipping: the brightest of the glare saturates at both framings, and the two sit over different parts of the globe's own brightness.

11. **The atmosphere shell's radius does not cross into the shader.** Step 1's list of new uniforms names "the globe circle and atmosphere radius". The radius went across for one round and no draw read it: the shader measures a fragment's height in the band against the globe's circle and the precomputed `sun_zone_width`, which is the annulus already turned into the unit every horizon effect is stated in, so the outer radius has nothing left to decide. It is now out of both `Uniforms` spellings, out of `write_uniforms`, out of `SunPlacement` and out of the `render_pipeline` mirrors and their offset test; the block is still 544 bytes, with a second pad where it sat. Validator round 1 is what found it.
