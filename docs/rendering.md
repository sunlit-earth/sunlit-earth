# Rendering

The frame from the shaders' point of view: the draw order first, then each layer of the sky and the clouds. The plumbing that feeds the renderer (the engine, `SceneParams`, texture slots, the resolution setting) is in [architecture.md](architecture.md). Each section records what was measured when the layer landed; the plans under `plans/` carry the full research behind it.

## Shaders

`shaders/blend.wgsl` and `shaders/sphere.wgsl` are concatenated at load time by `renderer/gpu_setup.rs`.

- `blend.wgsl`: `blend_fragment()` (day/night blending with diffuse shading and a per-channel `min(night, day)` clamp), plus `apply_gamma()` and `adjust_saturation()`.
- `sphere.wgsl`: star sprite and sphere vertex transforms, texture sampling, uniforms. Single-texture mode uses `terminator_width < 0` as a sentinel, and in that mode `fs_main` ignores the sun entirely and corrects the map with its own texture's gamma and saturation: the night's when bit 2 of `flags` says the night is drawn alone, the day's for the day map and the grid. The four shells over the globe take their `n_dot_l` from `shell_n_dot_l`, which is the Sun's own cosine, or 1.0 or -1.0 when bit 3 or bit 4 of `flags` asks for a globe lit evenly as day or as night; see The Day and Night surfaces. `schlick_fresnel()` drives both specular modulation and the diffuse color shift on ocean pixels. `fs_cloud` takes the map's clear-sky background off the one channel of the cloud texture, `R8Unorm` (architecture.md, The renderer), applies a floor and a gamma blended between land and ocean by the water mask, and shades the shell against its own geometry; see Haze removal, the latitude and the land weight, and Clouds on the night side. Three concentric atmosphere shells, each with its own vertex/fragment pair: `vs_rayleigh`/`fs_rayleigh` (radius ~1.015), `vs_nightglow_orange`/`fs_nightglow_orange` (~1.014), `vs_nightglow_green`/`fs_nightglow_green` (~1.015). `fs_rayleigh` carries the forward-scattering lobe as well as the shell's own blue, colored by `limb_hue_km` at the fragment's ray height, which is that transmission over its largest channel: the transmission itself at a surface-grazing ray is black, and what the band is is the hue that path leaves. The Sun is `vs_sun_disk`/`fs_sun_disk` for its body and `vs_sun_glare`/`fs_sun_glare` for the observer's glare, both quads generated from `vertex_index` alone; `fs_sun_disk` colors and fades itself through `limb_transmission`, the framebuffer-position form of the same function, which takes nothing about the Sun and is therefore callable from `fs_moon` when the Moon's own follow-up lands. The Moon is `vs_moon`/`fs_moon`, the sphere mesh through one model matrix and then `sky_lens_project`, which is the sky lens's forward projection factored out of `vs_star` when the Moon became its second consumer. The Milky Way is `vs_milky_way`/`fs_milky_way`, a screen quad that is always the whole frame, sampling the panorama through `milky_way_direction`, the inverse of `sky_lens_project` after `view_from_eqj`. Draw order: Milky Way, Stars and Planets, Sun disk, Moon, Earth, Clouds, Rayleigh, Nightglow Orange, Nightglow Green, Sun glare. The pass clears to (0.005, 0.005, 0.01), which is near black because there is a real sky on it now; three engine cases in `tests/engine/` hardcode the resulting pixel as `[1, 1, 3, 255]`.

## The globe's surface

`fs_main` reads the globe's surface through the equi-angular cube of plan decision 1 for every frame, whether it is drawn from the cube surface or from the grid. `equi_angular` turns the unit normal into the warped direction `w = atan(n / m) * 4 / pi`, with `m` the largest component's magnitude: that component comes out as exactly plus or minus one and the other two as the face's equi-angular coordinates, so the hardware's face selection and face coordinates are the ones the bake wrote, with the cube in the world frame (+Y north, +Z longitude 0, no rotation) and no per-face arithmetic in the shader. `w` is continuous across the face edges, so the implicit derivatives of a sample through it are sound, and every cube is read with `textureSample(cube, sampler, w)`: the day floor or the grid at binding 4, in blend mode the night floor at 5, and in every mode that draws from the cube the water mask at 6. Not with `textureSampleGrad` and the screen derivatives of `w`, which asks for the same sample: naga's MSL writer, in version 28 and in 30 alike, passes every explicit gradient as `metal::gradient2d`, which Metal accepts from a 2D texture or a 2D array and refuses from a cube, so the globe's pipeline does not build on macOS. The implicit derivatives are not bit for bit the explicit ones everywhere: on WARP the golden set comes out the same to the byte, on lavapipe (Mesa 23.2.1 in WSL) it moves by a mean of at most 0.11 and 3 steps a channel, and on this machine's Radeon (Vulkan) an 800 x 600 render of the shipped cube over Europe and Africa moves in 160 pixels, all on quads that straddle a face edge, by up to 5 steps on the surface and 14 on the limb, with no seam visible in either. `renderer::gpu_setup`'s `metal_is_never_asked_for_a_cube_gradient` translates the shader through naga's MSL backend on every platform and fails when a cube is sampled with `gradient2d`. The two branches in `globe_surface` are taken on uniforms alone, which keeps the samples in the uniform control flow `textureSample` needs; explicit gradients remain available for 2D textures and 2D arrays, such as the Milky Way's panorama and the tile array below. The mask is 255 over open water, and the Fresnel and specular terms read it as the share of open water. Blend mode falls back to the day floor alone until the night floor is resident. On a GPU every month's day floor stays resident once its pack has landed, on a CPU adapter the month in force's and the month ahead's, and binding 4 holds the month in force's, so a date in another month is drawn from its own floor in the frame that names it; only while the month in force's pack is still to be built is the floor drawn before it drawn instead ([architecture.md](architecture.md), Months). The mesh's equirectangular coordinates are read by the Moon and the cloud shell, and no longer by the globe's surface.

The grid is a cube of 512 px a face, generated at startup. Each texel takes the color the flat grid had at the place the mesh's coordinates put its direction, and a line covers a texel in proportion to how far into it the line reaches, over a ramp one texel wide: on the cube the lines cross the texel grid at every angle, where on the flat grid they ran along its rows and columns, and a texel that was either on a line or off it drew them as staircases at close zoom. The globe looks as it did apart from the poles, which are ordinary points now: the flat grid's meridians met in a pinch, and on the cube they meet at the middle of the +Y and -Y faces.

Every cube is read through one sampler, `renderer::surface_sampler_descriptor`: trilinear, anisotropy 8 on a GPU and 1 on a CPU adapter, the address mode moot. A second sampler binding in `fs_main` costs warp about 200 ms per 1080p frame whatever its state, and any anisotropy on a cube costs lavapipe seconds per frame where the frame crosses a face edge (plan departures 4 and 5, research section 19.2). The Moon, the Milky Way and the clouds keep the 16x sampler they had; a bind group decides which of the two sits at binding 2, so each shader still names one.

The formats follow the adapter (`SurfaceFormats`). On a GPU with `TEXTURE_COMPRESSION_BC` the floors are `Bc7RgbaUnorm` and the mask `Bc4RUnorm`, the pack's blocks uploaded as they are; on a CPU adapter the floors are decoded to `Rgba8Unorm` at upload with `tiles::decode_bc7` and the mask stays BC4; on an adapter without block compression the floors are decoded to RGBA8 and the mask to `R8Unorm` with `tiles::decode_bc4`. None of them is sRGB: the shader works on the stored values. `wgpu_init` requests `TEXTURE_COMPRESSION_BC` wherever the adapter offers it, and warp, lavapipe and GitHub's paravirtual Metal device all do (research section 19.1).

A render of the shipped faces on this machine's Radeon (Vulkan) and on warp, from a cache the first run built, draws the October floor on 30 September, past the middle of the month, and the July floor on 21 June, with no seam at the face edges or at the pole. The golden target runs its twenty-two reference cases and two companion tests in 3.9 s on warp in a debug build (2026-10-11), with the grid and the Earth fixture on the cube; it builds the fixture's fourteen packs before its engine starts, which a cache from an earlier run makes a read of each index.

Three cases in `tests/render_pipeline.rs` hold the warp to that. `the_warped_direction_is_continuous_across_every_face_edge` and `the_poles_are_ordinary_points_of_the_cube` read a cube that codes its own directions through production's `equi_angular` along every edge, around every corner and through both poles, and `the_globe_draws_no_seam_where_faces_meet` draws it through `fs_main` at every edge and corner, where a discontinuity in `w` or in its screen derivatives would show as a jump between pixels or as a coarse level read in the middle of the face. The golden case `africa_on_the_z_face` draws the golden suite's 256 px cube of the real Earth from over longitude 0 and latitude 0 and pins the orientation: Africa on +Z with north up. Their thresholds, and the mutations each was shown to fail, are in [testing.md](testing.md).

### The tiles above the floor

The floors are refined by 128 px tiles of the 1024 and 2048 levels (plan decision 4), read from one `texture_2d_array` at binding 7 through the page table at binding 8, both held by `renderer::tiles::SurfaceTiles`.

The array holds the day's tiles and the night's alike, each layer a tile with its 8 px gutter on every side, 144 px, and the one mip below it, 72 px. One array rather than one per surface is one binding and one budget: a mode that draws the day alone has every layer for the day. Its format is the floors' (`SurfaceFormats::color`): BC7 blocks as the pack stores them on a GPU with block compression, RGBA8 decoded with `tiles::decode_tile` on the loader's workers on a CPU adapter and on an adapter without block compression. It has `TILE_LAYER_BUDGET` layers, 900, on a GPU and `CPU_TILE_LAYER_BUDGET`, 640, on a CPU adapter, or the device's `max_texture_array_layers` where that is lower; the wanted set below measures the worst 4K frame at about 540 tiles in view for the day on a GPU and 390 on a CPU adapter, 665 and 496 in blend mode at the default shading, and up to 1,071 and 753 at the shading sliders' extremes, past both budgets, where the array takes the set in its order and the rest draws from above (plan departures 18 and 22, research section 24). It is created with the first tile uploaded and freed when the resolution setting allows none, so a renderer that draws no tile holds none of its 22.2 MiB in BC7 or 63.3 MiB in RGBA8.

The page table is an `R32Uint` array of six layers, one per face in cube layer order, with a texel per tile of the finest level, 16 x 16 on the shipped geometry. The low 16 bits of a texel say what the day draws in that cell and the high 16 bits what the night draws, so blend mode learns both from one `textureLoad`. Each half is a `PageEntry`: its kind in bits 14 and 15 (0 the floor, 1 constant ocean, 2 a tile), how many levels coarser than the finest its tile is in bits 12 and 13, which caps a geometry at four tiled levels (`MAX_TILED_LEVELS`, held by `Geometry::check`, which every pack build and `SurfaceTiles::new` run), and its layer in bits 0 to 11, which caps the array at 4096 layers. `PageTable::rewrite` rebuilds the whole table on the CPU from what is resident, no finer at each cell than the level a `CellLevels` cap names there: a cell names its tile of that level, else its nearest resident ancestor, where a tile the pack flags constant ocean counts as resident without a layer, else the floor, which is resident whenever a surface is drawn at all. A cap at the floor's level draws the floor whatever is resident, constant ocean included, since the floor holds the same flattened ocean. In a debug build it then checks its result with `PageTable::check`, which reads the layers from the other side, which tile a layer holds rather than where a tile is, so a layer freed or handed on without a rewrite shows up as a cell that names something that is not there. The table is uploaded with one `write_texture` after the writes of the tiles it names; the queue carries out every staged write before the next submit's commands, which is what keeps a reused layer from being read through the mapping of the tile it held before. The day half names the tiles of the month in force and no other month's (`SurfaceTiles::set_month`), from when that month's pack is handed over, which is when its floor is made resident; until then it draws the floor, so a month that changes while its pack builds shows the floor drawn before it without tiles, and no tile ever lies over another month's floor. With the night drawn alone the night floor sits at binding 4, and bit 2 of `flags` tells the shader to read the night half. The uniforms carry what a cell needs beyond its bits: the ocean colors of the day and the night packs as RGBA8 (`day_ocean`, `night_ocean`) and a tile's width and gutter in texels (`tile_texels`, `tile_gutter`); the cells per face come from the page table's own dimensions.

In `globe_surface` the floors are sampled first, and `dpdx(w)` and `dpdy(w)` taken, in the uniform control flow both need. `face_point` then finds the face the cube table selects for `w` and the coordinates on it, and projects the derivatives of `w` onto that face by the quotient rule, which is how the hardware takes a cube's own derivatives, so a tile is read over the footprint the floor's sample covers. The coordinates are clamped to the face: WGSL lets a division be off by 2.5 ulp, so at a face edge the quotient can land just past it, and the tile below 0 would be read at its far side. One load of the page table gives the cell, and `refined` returns the floor's sample, the pack's ocean color, or `textureSampleGrad` of the tile's layer at `(gutter + f * tile) / layer`, with `f` the place within the tile at its level and the face derivatives scaled by the tiles per side and `tile / layer`, through the surface sampler. The switch between the floor and a tile and between the two levels is continuous because both sides sample the same point with the same derivatives and each tile's gutter holds its neighbors' texels, those of the neighboring face across a face edge (the tile cache, [architecture.md](architecture.md)). On Metal that holds only to within the adapter's own rounding of a cube's level of detail: in `render_pipeline`'s level-coded minified case (CI run 36716684327) Metal's cube and its tile array, read through one isotropic sampler with the same derivatives, part by up to 97 steps of the mix between two levels, about 0.38 of a level, inside a face near its corner, where the Radeon, WARP and lavapipe part by 2 to 11. On the Earth fixture two neighboring levels differ by a mean of 2.7 steps a pixel at the framing of `a_packs_tiles_are_drawn_in_their_cells_as_the_texels_they_were_cut_from` (23216 over 8714 pixels), so 0.38 of a level comes to an estimated 1 step a pixel at a switch between the floor and a tile there, under the golden suite's mean tolerance of 2; nothing has measured it on real content on Metal (plan departure 21). Research section 8 sized the gutter for anisotropy 8: read at its own two levels, a tile's footprint reaches below nine texels at the first and half that at the second, and the rare one-texel overshoot past the 8 px gutter reads its clamped edge, which holds true neighbor data. A tile is read at those two levels only: a cell whose tile the camera has zoomed out past would read the tile's mip clamped and under-filtered, so the page table names nothing finer than the wanted set's cap (below, and plan departures 16 and 17). `SurfaceTiles` holds the cap, the finest level everywhere until `Renderer::set_tile_cap` hands it the wanted set's.

In the app the tile loader makes resident what the wanted set below asks for, and the page table is held to the preview's own cap before every preview frame and to an export's own for that export's frame ([architecture.md](architecture.md), The tile loader, and Readiness, the exports and the drag). Until a tile lands its cells draw what is resident above it: a coarser tile, the constant ocean color of a cell its pack flags, or the floor. The golden engine keeps a tile array, and each case's export waits for the tiles its own view wants and draws under its own cap, so a reference holds the tiles at the levels its frame asks for whatever the cases before it left resident, and comes out the same to the byte whether its case runs alone or after the others. Moving the engine onto the array moved the three cases on the Earth fixture on warp, `africa_on_the_z_face` by a mean of 1.24 with 954 of its 131072 pixels off by more than 24, `cloud_terminator_close_up` by 1.39 and 379, `clouds_across_the_terminator` by 0.66 and 507, and none of the others; so a render from the floors alone would still pass against these references, and the tiles' own drawing is held by the engine cases in testing.md. When the page table first drew constant ocean from the packs' indexes, that moved two of the golden suite's three cases on the Earth fixture and none of the others: 2592 of the 131072 pixels of `africa_on_the_z_face` by at most 2 steps a channel, a mean of 0.008, and 1748 of `clouds_across_the_terminator` by 1, so their references stayed. The tests of the tile path are in [testing.md](testing.md).

### The wanted set

`renderer::residency` decides which tiles a frame wants and the cap the page table takes, on the CPU from the cameras that draw the frame (plan decision 5), with no GPU, clock or thread: the same request gives the same set in the same order. A `Residency` measures every tile of a geometry once, its center direction, its angular radius to its corners, its four corners and the warp's largest stretch over it, 1.2 ms for the shipped 1,920. `Residency::wanted` takes the outputs (each a camera with its output's framing applied, as the render takes it, and its size in pixels), the month in force and the month ahead, if a hand-over is near, which surfaces are drawn, the finest level the resolution setting allows (`finest_level`: 8192 the 2048 level, 4096 the 1024 level, 2048 the floor's alone), whether a drag is in progress and its rate, the surface sampler's anisotropy (8 on a GPU, 1 on a CPU adapter), and a predicate that says whether a tile has a blob. It builds each camera with `OrbitalCamera::from_params`, the constructor `write_uniforms` uses, so the set and the picture agree on what is in the frame.

Each face's quadtree is descended from the coarsest tiled level, once per output. A tile is dropped where it lies wholly beyond the horizon the globe's mesh reaches, first by its cap (the angle from the eye's direction to its center against that horizon plus its radius) and, where the cap is not decisive, by the exact nearest point of its four great-circle edges; and where its bounding ball lies outside the frame's four side planes by more than `MARGIN_TILES`, one tile width. The mesh's horizon is a little past the sphere's `acos(1 / d)`: the mesh's facets are chords, and a facet whose plane faces the eye can have corners, and fragments interpolated toward them, up to `acos(cos r / d) + r` from the point under the eye, `r` the circumradius of the largest facet, which `geometry::sphere::facet_radius` derives from `GLOBE_STACKS` and `GLOBE_SECTORS`, the counts `gpu_setup` builds the mesh with: about 3.2 degrees past the sphere's. A tile is wanted where the level below it projects a texel to more than `THRESHOLD_PX`, 1 px, or `DRAG_THRESHOLD_PX`, 2 px, while a drag is in progress; a pending export's own view keeps 1 px during a drag, since it is published once that set is resident. The level below the coarsest tiled level is the floor, so a tile of the 1024 level is wanted only where the floor's own texel is too coarse, and at the far end of the zoom nothing is (plan departure 17). A wanted tile whose children in view are all wanted gives way to them, so the set holds the level the rule gives at each point and not the ancestors above it: with a tile a positional read of 26 KB and no decode, coarse before fine buys little, and the ancestors would add about a quarter to the peak. Tiles with no blob are left out, since the page table draws them from the pack's index.

The projected texel is counted as the sampler's level of detail counts it, and bounded rather than measured. Where the sampler's anisotropy covers the texel's elongation, the level of detail follows the texel's longest side, which the rule bounds by the warp's largest stretch over the tile (up to 15% more than at a face center), the tile's nearest depth (its ball's), and the perspective's largest stretch at the tile's widest angle off the lens axis (clamped to the frame's corner, `sqrt(1 + tan^2)` over the depth). Where foreshortening elongates the texel past what the anisotropy covers, the level of detail follows the shorter side times the anisotropy; the shorter side is at most the longer times the cosine between the view ray and the surface, which is largest where the tile is nearest the point under the eye, so the rule takes the longer side's bound times the anisotropy times that cosine when that is less. Every bound errs toward the finer level, so each pixel gets at least the level its own footprint asks for, which the tests check by ray cast at both anisotropies. The other way round it is an approximation: a tile is judged at its worst point, so its farther or more oblique parts read its coarser level past what they would need. On a GPU that is 19% of the tiled pixels at the zoom where the set is largest, all but 0.1% of them by less than a level; on a CPU adapter, whose sampler has no anisotropy, 18% to 44% between 5.76 and 9.4 radii, all but 0.9% by less than a level, at worst 3.4 levels (research section 23, plan departure 20). A ray cast that counts, for every second pixel, the tile of the level its own footprint asks for finds within 1% of the tiles the descent wants on a GPU at 5.76 and 8 radii and 9% fewer at 9.4, and the slack grows where the set is small.

The margin is one ring round the frame: a tile within one tile width of the frame, at its own level, is wanted as margin. It rings the frame and not the visible set, so nothing past the horizon is prefetched; during a drag, cameras along the drag's lead, `DRAG_LEAD_STEPS` of them spaced to `DRAG_LEAD_SECONDS` of its rate ahead, add what they see to the margin, which is what brings in the tiles a drag turns over the limb (plan departure 19). The set is ordered by what the frame lacks: the tiles in view of a pending export first, then every other tile in view, then the margin, then the month ahead's day tiles in view, one for each day tile of the month in force in view, marked as margin so that they never count toward readiness; within each, the largest level deficit first, the levels between the floor and the tile's own, then the nearest the middle of the frame, over the outputs that see the tile. The deficit is counted against the floors alone; a loader that knows what else is resident may rank by the levels actually missing. A tile wanted by several outputs is in the set once. In blend mode a tile wants the day's pack where the sun's term `n . l` exceeds `-terminator_width` somewhere on it, and the night's where it is below `terminator_width` or, with diffuse shading on and its floor below 1, below the ramp: `blend_fragment` holds the shaded day at or above `min(night, day)` wherever the shading darkens it, so the night decides pixels there on the day side too.

The cap is the finest level wanted at each cell for what is in view, and the floor's level where nothing is. `Wanted::cap` is the finest over every output of the request; `Residency::cap` gives one output's alone, and each render takes its own (plan departure 23). It is taken at 1 px even while a drag asks for 2 px, in a second descent: a resident tile of the 1 px level serves the frame within the approximation above however little of the set the drag asks for, while a tile one level finer would be under-filtered by up to a factor of two more. One computation for a 4K output takes 0.02 ms at the near end of the zoom, 0.11 ms where the set is largest and 0.02 ms at the far end, 0.29 ms during a drag, and 0.12 ms in blend mode at the defaults, in a release build on this machine (research section 23).

### Sea ice in the day faces

The sea ice is part of the picture, like the Blue Marble's own snow: the texture pipeline's `cube` command generates each month's ice from the monthly mean concentration of 2004 in EUMETSAT OSI SAF's OSI-450-a1 and composites it over the open water of that month's faces before the downsample (`textures/PROVENANCE.md`, and the plan [plans/2026-10-11-baked-sea-ice-plan.md](plans/2026-10-11-baked-sea-ice-plan.md)). What the ice looks like is the pipeline's alone, and its second iteration ([plans/2026-10-12-sea-ice-look-plan.md](plans/2026-10-12-sea-ice-look-plan.md)) changed only the faces: the closed pack is ridged relief, the leads thin jagged fractures, the open water whole cells read through a tone curve that closes the pack from 85%, every gap the ocean fill; the sea within 10 km of land is ice where the concentration is half or more, the coast's flat snowy land on `py` and `ny` is blended toward the ice, and the mask takes the Blue Marble's own water poleward of 50 degrees. The renderer adds no slot, binding, uniform or setting for it. The ice gets what the land has, the faces' resolution, the codec, the BC7 transcode, the tiles and the mip chains, and it changes with the month's floor and tiles at the mid-month hand-over ([architecture.md](architecture.md), Months). The night faces have none: the Black Marble draws the ice sheets and snowy land a dim blue, the Greenland ice sheet about (41, 48, 80), but every sea, frozen or not, as one flat (5, 5, 15), so blend mode draws the ice on the night side from the day texel (below). It replaces the first plan's ice, which a shader generated in screen space so that the pack changed shape with the zoom ([plans/2026-10-10-sea-ice-plan.md](plans/2026-10-10-sea-ice-plan.md), on `feature/sea-ice`, not merged).

Three things in the renderer know about it, and all three know it only by brightness over the mask's water, the water term and the night side only at the latitudes the caps reach, since after the bake a texel the mask calls open water is the flattened fill, (10, 30, 60) and the codec's noise, or ice.

- **The water term.** `fs_main` scales the mask's share of water by one minus `ice`, before the specular glint and the Fresnel shift read it. `ice` is `smoothstep(ICE_LUMA_FOOT, ICE_LUMA_TOP, dot(surface.day, LUMA))`, 52 and 76 of 255 on the day texel's stored values with Rec. 709 weights, times `smoothstep(ICE_LATITUDE_FOOT, ICE_LATITUDE_TOP, |latitude|)`, 30 and 35 degrees, with the latitude of the normal the cube is sampled with. Open water decodes at most 42.9 in luminance and 46.0 after the BC7 transcode, and fully covered ice at least 76 decoded and 69 after BC7 (plan departure 8), so open water keeps both effects, ice at 76 or above draws matte, and ice the transcode puts lower keeps a little of them, about 21% of the water term at 69; a texel ice covers in part keeps part of them. The latitude gate is there because a bright texel over water is not always ice: at a coast the texel holds the land's share too, and a sand or snow coast is bright. The bake puts ice only within its caps, which reach 35 degrees, so equatorward of 30 degrees every coast keeps its glint, and between 30 and 35 the term fades in. Poleward of 35 degrees a bright coast without ice still loses some of its glint: 15 to 19% of the coast texels there on the first bake's January and July faces, snowy coasts in winter and bright shores (plan departure 15). The second look leaves the ramp emptier than the first: of the mask's open water poleward of 35 degrees, 0.17% and 0.19% of the texels of `py` in March and August and 0.10% and 0.18% of `ny` in February and September decode between 52 and 76, against 0.23%, 0.29%, 0.13% and 0.25% on the first bake's faces, since every gap between the floes is the fill and the pack is brighter. The term reads the texel the picture is drawn from, so it fades with the ice at every level of the mip chain. A cell the pack flags constant ocean returns the exact ocean color and keeps its glint. The single-surface modes draw no glint at all, so nothing changes there.
- **The night side.** In blend mode `fs_main` draws the ice into the night texel before the night's gamma and saturation, so it takes the corrections the ice sheets beside it take. Its color is `NIGHT_ICE`, (41, 49, 81) of 255 on the stored values, the Black Marble's Greenland ice sheet, times the day texel's luminance over 240, the winter pack's, up to one, so thin ice and the edge stay darker as they are by day. The weight is the water term's `ice` times the mask's share of water, and `night_with_ice` puts the ice in place of the water's share of the night pack's ocean color, `night + weight * (ice - fill)`, as the bake composites the day's (plan departure 4), kept between the night texel and the ice. So a coast's land and its lights keep their color: mixing the whole night texel toward the ice instead dimmed the coast texels poleward of 30 degrees whose day texel is bright, 4120 of them lit above 100 in luminance on the shipped January faces, by up to 133 levels, and St. Petersburg's lights on the frozen Gulf of Finland by up to 117 in a render of March, and left a quarter of the fill at ice-bound coasts half over water. The bound keeps water that is not the fill, lights at sea, from being brightened past both. The night ice has less detail than the day's, for two reasons. It is one hue scaled by brightness, so the day's tone by season keeps only what the luminance carries. The second look's brighter winter colors put the Antarctic's winter pack past the 240 at which the scale stops: in a render of July over the open water poleward of 70 S its night ice went from (40.9, 47.4, 73.0) to (42.6, 49.6, 76.3) as its day luminance went from 229 to 243, while the Arctic's in January, at 242 and 247, stayed within 0.3 of (41.7, 48.7, 75.4). And the residency wants day tiles only where the day decides pixels, the cells whose highest `n . l` exceeds `-terminator_width` (`residency::drawn`), so further into the night the day texel that scales and gates the ice comes from the 512 px floor, about 20 km a texel, beside night land drawn from 2048 px tiles: there the leads and the floes' gaps are averaged away, and the ice's sharpness changes at cell borders as the terminator moves. Wanting day tiles on the night side poleward of 30 degrees in blend mode would restore them, at a cost in tiles. The night drawn alone reads no day texel, so the Night mode draws no sea ice (plan departure 17); it reads the mask since the ocean colors, for the night's color alone (The ocean color, below).
- **The day tiles.** A day tile is flagged constant ocean only where its whole footprint on the finest mask is open water and every texel of it, gutter included, lies within `DAY_OCEAN_TOLERANCE`, 8, of the pack's ocean color, the rule the night pack applies with 4 for lights at sea; the ocean color is the median per channel of the texels the mask calls open water, not their mean, which a winter month would pull toward white (`assets::tiles::build`). ## The Day and Night surfaces

The Rendering group's Surface combo offers Grid, Day, Night and Day/Night Blend. Day and Night show one map lit evenly, and everything drawn over the globe follows: `write_uniforms` sets bit 3 of `flags` for Day and bit 4 for Night through `render_pass::shell_lighting_flags`, and `shell_n_dot_l` then gives `fs_cloud`, `fs_rayleigh` and both nightglow shells 1.0 or -1.0 in place of the Sun's cosine. No uniform field was added, so the block's layout is untouched. The shells' existing curves do the rest. At 1.0 the clouds' `sunlit` is 1, so the whole deck is day clouds at the day opacity; Rayleigh's `day_t` is 1, its terminator band `exp(-n_dot_l^2 / 0.008)` is `exp(-125)` and its night fade 0, so the shell is its day blue all the way round; and both nightglow masks are 0. At -1.0 the clouds' `sunlit` is 0, so the deck is `cloud_night` at the night opacity everywhere; Rayleigh's night fade is 1, which leaves the extinction and no color, as on the night side; and both nightglow masks are 1 at the midnight time of night, half strength for the orange layer and full for the green.

The Rayleigh shell's forward-scattering lobe is gated off in both modes by `shells_follow_the_sun`. It is a terminator effect, the sunlit limb seen against the Sun, and it has its own gate on `n_dot_l` that 1.0 would open everywhere. The surface's specular glint and Fresnel shift need nothing: they sit after `fs_main`'s single-texture return, so no single-texture mode reaches them.

The grid keeps the shells under the real Sun. It carries the sentinel as Day and Night do, which is why the sentinel cannot say which lighting the shells get, and a grid under a real terminator is what makes it useful for checking where the Sun is; it is also what the golden suite pictures. The blend has a real terminator and keeps it. The Sun's disk and glare are sky objects, drawn in every mode as before, and the Moon keeps its own phase lighting, since `fs_moon` reads `sun_dir` directly.

The lighting follows the mode the user selected, `TextureMode::from_index(params.texture_index)`, and not the bind group `texture_routing::resolve_textures` resolved. Those differ while cubes load: Day and Night draw the grid until their floor is resident, and the blend draws the day floor alone until the rest of what it blends is. A mode switch therefore reaches the shells at once and the surface catches up as its cubes land, and the blend's loading fallback keeps its Sun-driven shells. The surface's own sentinel and `FLAG_NIGHT_ALONE` keep following the resolved group. The cases are `the_even_modes_light_every_shell_the_same_on_both_hemispheres` and `the_even_modes_draw_no_sunrise_lobe` in `tests/render_pipeline.rs`, and `day_and_night_light_the_clouds_evenly` in `tests/engine/clouds.rs`. The plan is [plans/2026-10-10-surface-mode-lighting-plan.md](plans/2026-10-10-surface-mode-lighting-plan.md).

## The ocean color

Two settings, `day_ocean_color` and `night_ocean_color`, set the base color of open water on the day map and on the night map, before gamma and saturation ([plans/2026-10-18-ocean-color-plan.md](plans/2026-10-18-ocean-color-plan.md)). The textures keep their fills, and the shader moves the open water's share of each texel from the fill the bake gave it to the chosen color: `recolor_ocean` is `texel + open_water * (chosen - fill)`, clamped to the unit cube for `apply_gamma`, with `open_water` the mask's share of water less the share the ice term takes. It is the arithmetic `night_with_ice` uses to put ice in place of the night's fill, so on the blend's night side the water's share becomes ice by `ice` and the chosen color by `1 - ice`. A coast texel half over water moves halfway, a light at sea keeps its excess over the fill, and ice keeps its color.

**The fill.** It is the ocean color of the pack the drawn floor came from. `install_surface` keeps `Pack::ocean()` beside each resident cube, `ResidentCube::ocean`, the floor and its pack's tile index arriving in the same call, and `Renderer::fills` takes the day floor's and the night floor's for the group the frame draws with (`surface::drawn_fills`), none for the grid. `render_pass::ocean_shift` makes `(chosen - fill) / 255` per channel, and `write_uniforms` writes the day's and the night's as `day_ocean_shift` and `night_ocean_shift`, two `vec3` uniforms with their padding after `_pad11`, the block now 608 bytes. The shift is exactly zero with no floor drawn, with a fill of alpha zero, on the grid, and when the chosen color is the fill, and `fs_main` applies each in a branch on the uniform being non zero, so at the default a fragment runs the arithmetic it ran before. Measuring from the floor's pack rather than from `day_ocean` avoids the window after a month change in which the month in force's pack has not reached the tiles and `day_ocean` reads zero, which would recolor by the whole chosen color. A constant ocean cell draws the pack's color, which the shift takes exactly to the chosen one; a floor texel or a stored tile holds the fill within the codec's noise and lands within the same few levels of it.

**Which shift where.** In Day mode the day's, on the day texel, by `water * (1 - ice)`; the ice term is computed before the single-surface return now, the same expression blend mode uses, so the picker does not tint the ice the bake composited into the day faces. In Night mode the night's, on the night texel, by the mask's whole share of water: the night drawn alone has no day texel and so no ice, and the Black Marble's frozen seas are the same flat fill as its open ones, so they take the chosen night color. In blend mode the day's on the day texel and the night's on the night texel after `night_with_ice`. The constant ocean cells, the ice gate, `NIGHT_ICE`, the Fresnel shift's sky color and `fs_cloud` are untouched, and the water term the glint and the Fresnel shift read is the mask's and the original day texel's, so both act on recolored water as on the fill.

**The mask in every cube mode.** `surface::needs` asks for the mask in Day, Night and blend mode, and `rebuild_surface_groups` binds it in the Day and Night groups in place of the dummy, so a Day or Night publish waits for it as a blend one does. The cost is the mask's cube, about 4 MiB in BC4 at 1024 px with its chain, which the blend default loaded already. The grid keeps its own group with the dummy mask. Loading the mask only while a color differs from its fill was the alternative, and was not taken: the fill is known only once a floor is resident, so the mask would come in a second phase, and readiness would change with a setting.

**The defaults.** They are the shipped fills, (10, 30, 60) by day, `#0a1e3c`, and (5, 5, 15) by night, `#05050f`, stored in the config through `color::Rgb8`, which writes `#rrggbb` in lower case and reads it with or without the `#` and in either case; a malformed value fails the file as any value of the wrong type does. `the_real_packs_carry_the_default_ocean_colors` holds every shipped pack to the defaults, so a rebake that moves a fill fails it, and the step that follows is `change_default` in `config/migrate.rs` for the users who kept the old default. The Earth fixture's day packs carry (11, 30, 60), a level redder, so the golden suite draws at `test_support::EARTH_FIXTURE_OCEAN` and asserts it when it builds the packs.

**The limits.** The mask is a texel of about 10 km, twice the finest day texel, so at the closest zoom a band about one mask texel wide along a coast is recolored by the mask's share rather than the texel's: land takes a little of the chosen color, and water keeps a little of the fill. It is invisible at the default colors and grows with the distance between the chosen color and the fill. Bright coasts poleward of 30 degrees lose some of their water's share to the ice term, as their glint does, and are recolored less. Water the mask marks that the bake did not flatten keeps its own texture and moves by the same shift; whether the large lakes show it is for the look with the user.

**The settings.** Color Correction's Day Texture and Night Texture headings each start with an Ocean row, `SettingColor` in `widgets.slint`: the label, a swatch, the hex text and a reset, on `SettingRow`'s contract (`value`, `default-value`, `modified`, `changed`), so the headings' and the group's resets cover it through `TextureColorSettings.ocean` with no code of their own. The swatch's accessible value is the hex text and setting it reads one. Clicking it opens `ColorPicker` in a `PopupWindow` that closes on a click outside: a saturation and value square over the hue, a hue strip, and a hex field that takes `#rrggbb` or `rrggbb` on Enter, ignores anything else and shows the color again when it loses focus. The square's vertical axis is the square root of value, drawn with a black layer of nine stops at `1 - (1 - y)^2`, since both defaults are dark, at a value of 0.24 and 0.06, which a linear axis puts in the square's bottom quarter and bottom sixteenth. The picker takes hue, saturation and value from the color each time it opens, keeps its own hue while saturation or value is zero, and writes the color from its pointer and edit handlers alone, so a color nobody touches never passes through a float round trip. Slint 1.18 has no color picker and nothing in its language formats or parses hex, so the `ColorText` global's two pure callbacks are implemented in the app with `Rgb8`'s own functions (`ui_callbacks::register_color_text`), which the app and the UI tests both call. Slint's winit backend draws a popup inside the window rather than as a window of its own, placed and kept within the window by the core's `place_popup`, so the picker is not clipped by the sidebar on any platform; in an offscreen render through Slint's software renderer a pick at the square's middle set (32, 46, 64) where the square draws (31, 45, 64).

## Celestial sky

`scene::sky::SkyState` is the one astronomy result for each frame. From the selected datetime it builds the J2000 equatorial to world rotation, rotates a geocentric Astronomy Engine sun vector, computes directions and apparent magnitudes for Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn, and places the Moon: a position in Earth radii from `Astronomy_GeoMoon` and a rotation from `Astronomy_RotationAxis`. The Moon is a position rather than a direction because it is the one thing in the sky near enough for the camera's own displacement to matter. The engine passes the whole state to the renderer and the dirty check compares the sun plus two quantized rotation basis vectors. The older subsolar calculation in `scene::sun` remains as an independent equivalence test.

`cargo xtask bake stars` reads HYG v4.4, excludes its `Sol` row, propagates proper motion to epoch 2026.0, bakes B minus V color and magnitude, and writes 16 byte records after a 12 byte header. `assets::stars` validates the embedded blob and lends its payload directly to wgpu as the static instance buffer. The blob with 15,597 stars and its `ATTRIBUTION.md` ship together under `crates/sunlit-core/src/assets/stars/`; xtask does not depend on `sunlit-core`. The format contract between the two crates is held by a fixture in `crates/sunlit-core/tests/fixtures/`, a CSV and a BIN checked in side by side: the reader's tests load the BIN, and `the_committed_fixture_matches_a_fresh_bake` in xtask bakes the CSV and compares, so a layout change that lands on one side and not the other fails there rather than in whatever the sky looked like afterwards.

The star pipeline draws over the Milky Way's panorama and under everything else. Four generated triangle strip vertices expand every catalog record into an analytic sprite with a crisp core and independently controlled glow, transformed by the shared sky rotation and camera rotation. The glow is a Gaussian with the value at `star_glow_radius` subtracted and the remainder renormalized, so it reaches zero exactly where the sprite quad ends: a bare Gaussian still carries 4.4% of its peak there, and with brightness, glow strength and glow radius all at their maxima that residual draws the quad's own edge as a straight line and puts bright stars in visible squares. `golden_bright_star_halos` is that corner of the parameter space, and `large_crisp_stars` is the other end of the same axis with no halo at all. Earth retains its own perspective lens, `camera_fov`, defaulting to the 20 degrees the presets were framed at; celestial directions use a separate stereographic lens with its own `sky_fov`. Records are sorted by magnitude, so the runtime submits only the prefix inside the selected limit; the shader keeps the same cutoff as a boundary check. Brightness uses compressed astronomical flux. A second buffer with five records carries the planets through the same pipeline, rewritten by the two calls that move the renderer's stored sky and by neither of them when it did not move: the buffer is what the draw reads and the stored inputs are what a replayed export re-encodes, so the two have to name the same instant. Intensity zero omits both draws. Earth then covers the sky, followed by clouds and the atmosphere shells.

## The Sun

The Sun is two draws, because it is two things at once. Its body is a celestial object, so the clipped white disk at its true 0.267 degree angular radius, times whatever `sun_size` asks for, is drawn with the sky, after the stars and before the Earth, where the opaque globe covers whatever falls inside its painted disc. Its glare forms in the observer rather than in the scene, so it is a second quad drawn last over everything, faded by the fraction of the disk the globe leaves visible rather than by whether the disk survived a depth test. One draw could be occluded or overlaying, not both. Both are four vertices generated from `vertex_index` with no vertex buffer, and `render_pass::Sun::select` chooses them the way `Stars::select` chooses the star draw; `sun_glow` at zero takes the Sun out of the scene entirely, body included.

Both quads are sized through the sky lens by the conformal cone-to-disc formula, which is exact rather than a small-angle approximation: stereographic projection maps circles on the sphere to circles in the plane, so a cone of half-angle alpha about a direction at angle theta images as a disc spanning `tan((theta - alpha) / 2)` to `tan((theta + alpha) / 2)` along the radial direction, the first signed so a cone containing the view axis straddles the origin with no special case. The projected plane reaches pixels by one uniform scale, so that disc is a circle in pixels whatever the aspect ratio is. A draw is skipped when the cone's near edge is past the screen corner's own angle, and drawn fullscreen when the cone reaches the antipode, where its image is the exterior of a circle; there is no behind-the-camera case, because the sky lens has no `w` and maps every direction short of the antipode.

`scene::sun_occlusion` is the CPU half, and it measures against the silhouette the viewer can see rather than the true angular limb. The mixed lens puts the painted globe about four and a half times larger on screen than the sky lens's image of the same silhouette at the two default fields of view, so measuring against the second would fade the glare out while the Sun still sits in visibly empty sky in one framing and leave it burning on top of the painted globe in another. In screen space all three shapes are circles: the disk from the cone formula, the globe at `r / sqrt(d^2 - r^2)` normalized by the camera's own field of view, and the atmosphere shell at the same formula with `RAYLEIGH_RADIUS`. The visible fraction is the disk's area outside the globe circle, by the standard circle-circle lens area. The cost is that the answer does not agree with an ephemeris, which is deliberate and is why `angular_visible_fraction` sits beside it as the ephemeris answer nothing reads: the tests compare the two in the one configuration where the lenses agree about scale, which fixes the aspect ratio once both fields of view are chosen. The functions live where the uniforms are written rather than in `SkyState`, because they are a function of the lens and the viewport as well as of the time.

The horizon zone is the unit every effect at the limb is measured in. `sun_occlusion::horizon_zone_width` is the painted annulus, or `sun_horizon_depth` disk diameters where that is wider, in pixels; the annulus is a pixel or two on a preview at the default zooms, so the default of one diameter is what makes a gradient across the disk exist there at all. One zone width stands for the shell's own 96 km, which is what turns a height on screen into a height in the atmosphere, and `limb_transmission` turns that into what the path left of the light: `70 * exp(-h / 7 km) * sun_reddening` air masses through Mallama's per-channel `(0.90, 0.836, 0.73)`. The function exists once in Rust and once in `sphere.wgsl`, which is why the shared-rule probe now pins three rules rather than two. `fs_sun_disk` reads it per fragment, so the disk carries a gradient rather than a tint: normalized to its largest channel for the color, and faded by `sqrt(saturate(T_green * 40))` below about nine kilometers, so it goes out where its light does instead of being cut where the painted globe begins.

The glare's color and its amplitude are both integrals over the visible disk. `integrate_disk` walks strips of constant distance from the globe's center, which is the direction the band's height is measured along, and returns the mean hue weighted by what of the disk is drawn together with the mean transmission. The glare draws `sun_glow * X * F^0.5`, where F is the visible fraction times that transmission and the square root is Stevens' point-source exponent standing in for the HDR pipeline decision 7 declined: a disk carrying a hundredth of its light still glares at a tenth of full strength, which is what first light looks like. X is `exposure_gain`, which holds at `sun_horizon_boost` while the disk's lower edge is inside the zone and settles to 1 over `sun_horizon_reach` zone widths above it, an eye's adaptation lag with altitude standing in for time. The disk itself takes no gain, being clipped already. `a_sliver_of_sun_over_the_limb_still_glares` and `the_glare_peaks_as_the_disk_clears_the_horizon` are the engine cases, and both measure the light the Sun adds to a frame rather than its brightest pixel: the disk is drawn clipped white wherever it is drawn at all, so a brightest pixel is 254 at every exposure.

Refraction is a lift and a squash in the same units, behind one slider. `refract` maps a geometric height in zone widths to the apparent one through `u = a - 0.46 exp(-13.7 a)`, inverted in a few Newton steps, and the vertical magnification is that map's slope inverted. `place_sun` moves the disc's center radially by the lift in pixels and inverts the sky lens analytically to recover the direction that lands there, so the disk, the glare and every angular falloff move together with one formula between them; a lift of zero skips the round trip, which is what keeps a Sun the band cannot reach bit-exact. The squash reaches `visibility` as a limb moved by the same factor rather than as an ellipse, because scaling along the cut's normal preserves area fractions where the limb is a line. That is exact where the disk sits and curves away from the truth to either side of it: 0.025 of the visible fraction at the true half degree, 0.065 at three times it and 0.121 at eight, worst where the disk is deepest in the band and its light nearly gone. `a_squashed_disk_against_the_limb_is_a_round_one_against_a_moved_limb` holds that to 0.03, which is why its 400 by 400 sampling grid is not free to fall: halving it would eat the remaining headroom.

The exponential air-mass fit gives up further down. `70 exp(-h / 7)` reproduces Mallama's cumulative air masses to about a fifth over the whole band, and transmission is exponential in air mass, so a fifth at the top of the band is a factor of one and a half at the bottom: twenty percent holds only where the air mass is small. At 13 km the model is 16 percent short of the table's 13 air masses, which comes out as 41 percent more green, so `the_transmitted_green_follows_the_table_where_the_air_mass_is_small` asserts the first six rows within 20 percent and pins the 13 km row separately at 1.412 times the table's 0.10.

The warm band along the limb is the same light path on the atmosphere shell. `fs_rayleigh` carries a Henyey-Greenstein forward lobe, colored by the hue `limb_transmission` leaves at the fragment's own ray height and gated on the fragment being sunlit; `atmo_sunrise_glow` and `atmo_sunrise_width` sit in the Atmosphere group because the effect is the atmosphere's. Plain Henyey-Greenstein keeps 3.3 percent of its peak at every backscattering angle, which is a warm ring around the limb of every frame whose Sun is behind the camera rather than a lobe around anything, so the floor is subtracted and the remainder renormalized, which is what the star glow's Gaussian does at its own edge. With it, `default` and `rayleigh` moved by 0.0002 and 0.009 of a level; without it both move by several.

The lobe's scattering angle is the composite's rather than the scene's: between the fragment's own sky-lens ray, recovered with the `sky_lens_direction` the glare and the panorama already call, and the Sun's image. It began as the true angle between the fragment's view ray and the Sun's real direction, which is the one place the first amendment left phase B's decision 2, and the two frames disagree by tens of degrees at a close camera: at 3.7 Earth radii the true limb is 15.68 degrees off the view axis while the Sun's image reaches the painted limb only at 58.3, so the band peaked with the image still deep inside the painted disc and was a quarter of its peak by the time the disk was drawn. `atmo_sunrise_width` keeps its number and its hint and means the sky-lens angle at which the lobe falls to half, which is what makes the band a fixed width in pixels at every zoom the way the glare is. Measured at 512 by 256 and 140 degrees of sky, the framing whose Sun has no image near the limb adds 381 of red excess to a frame against the 31221 of the one whose image stands on it, where the true angle gave 1287 against 12222; `the_sunrise_band_arrives_with_the_suns_image` is that case and `sunrise_band_close` is the golden beside it.

The composition is the Spencer glare model, per fragment, in angular coordinates: the fragment shader inverts the sky lens analytically to recover its view ray, measures the angle to the Sun, and sums a two-lobe inverse-power bloom with the existing `dither()`, ciliary corona needles from an integer hash, and a lenticular halo near three degrees with a blue inner and red outer edge. Measuring in degrees rather than pixels is what makes the composition hold as the sky lens widens. The composition was built and reviewed against 60 to 180 degrees across the width, which the slider covered when it was measured there; its 30 to 120 across the short edge draws 52.7 to 183 across a 16:9 width; a spanned canvas derives a wider sky than any slider position, up to the shader's own 330, and `panorama_at_a_wide_sky` is the only reference out there. One angular window takes every sun-centered term to zero before its quad ends, which is the same lesson `star_halo_profile` carries: a term still carrying anything where its quad stops draws that quad's own edge as a line. The needle hash is a PCG integer hash rather than the usual `fract(sin(...))`, because that one feeds a transcendental a large argument where adapters disagree in the low bits and needles are exactly the high-frequency detail that turns such a disagreement into a golden failure; the needle count also comes down rather than aliasing when a tip would be narrower than two pixels. Angular sizes become pixel sizes as the slider moves, so the core takes the same treatment phase A gave the star core: a minimum radius in pixels and the shared `output_pixel_scale` ramp from 1.0 at 1080p to 2.0 at 4K.

`sun_flare` is camera mode: an aperture starburst and three ghosts along the axis from the Sun through the frame's center, which is a device artifact rather than an eye's and reads as smudges in a still. It ships at 0.15, `AppConfig::sanitize` clamps it to the slider's 1 for the reason `camera_fov` is clamped, and `base_params` and `test_params` pin it to zero, because the spikes and the ghosts reach outside the glare's own cone and a default-on flare would put streaks in every reference and every pixel count with a Sun near the frame. It is the one thing that makes the glare quad fullscreen, because ghosts land outside the glare cone, so at the default `fs_sun_glare` runs over every pixel of every frame. Measured against the same frame with the flare at zero, alternating the two so the machine's own drift cancels, that costs 16 ms at 1920 by 1080 on `warp` and 60 ms at 3840 by 2160, which is the ratio four times the pixels should give a fullscreen fragment shader, against the panorama's 133 and 555, and nothing separable from noise on this machine's GPU, where it is zero within 3 ms. Those three are differences between two otherwise identical export renders, timed around the readback; no frame total is recorded, because the ones that measurement printed did not scale with the pixel count and were not rechecked. The cone-sized quad the flare replaces costs nothing measurable at all: at the default sky field of view its 30 degrees are a twenty-fifth of the frame.

Four goldens pin it, all at 60 degrees of sky rather than the 140 degree default: the field of view decides how many pixels a degree is worth, and 512 of them across 140 degrees is three, so at the default the whole composition lands inside forty pixels of a 512 pixel frame and a reference that lost the Sun entirely still passes at a mean of 1.11 with half a percent of outliers, against a tolerance of 2.00. At 60 degrees a degree is eight pixels and the glare is most of the frame. `sun_over_the_night_side` and `sun_grazing_the_limb` are the composition clear of the limb and grazing it. `sun_rising_through_the_band` is the disk crossing the painted limb inside the zone, and it carries four times the Sun's size for the reason `moon_crescent` carries eight times the Moon's: at its true half degree the disk is 4.6 pixels across, the zone is the same 4.6, and the part of the band that colors anything is a pixel and a half of that. `sunrise_band` is the shell's lobe with the Sun behind the limb. Both new cases compare a window rather than the frame, the first because deleting the refraction moves the whole frame by a mean of 0.91 against a tolerance of 2.00 and the second because the band is a thread down a limb the grid texture already paints bright. The engine cases carry the default field of view instead, where a count of painted pixels needs no tolerance.

Three things about the Sun are true on both sides of the CPU and GPU boundary, and none of them is visible to a frame the rest of the suite renders. The density ramp, the sky lens's edge radius and the limb transmission exist once in `scene::sky_lens` and `scene::limb_extinction` and once in `sphere.wgsl`, and `the_shader_and_the_cpu_agree_on_the_three_shared_rules` appends a compute entry point to the production shaders and reads all three back off the GPU: the first two at viewport heights away from 1080p, because that is where the ramp clamps to 1.0 and every other frame in the suite is rendered, and the third at nine heights through the band at three reddening settings, because what a golden can see of it is one disk a few pixels across. The pan reaches the Sun through four places with a sign in each, and every other frame in the suite is rendered with no pan at all: `a_pan_slides_the_composite_without_shearing_it` pins the three that place something by rendering a frame panned by an eighth of the width and comparing it against the unpanned one moved by 64 pixels, and `a_pan_past_the_frame_corner_still_draws_the_sun` covers the fourth, the reach a draw culls itself against, at a longitude where the Sun sits more than the glare cone's own 30 degrees past an unpanned frame's corner, which is the only place that reach decides anything. A draw's cone is its own angular radius, which for the disk is the true half degree times `sun_size`, so `an_enlarged_disk_reaching_the_frame_corner_is_drawn` sits beside it at the one band of directions where that factor decides anything: an eight times disk whose crescent is inside the corner and whose true-sized image would be wholly outside it.

## The Moon

The Moon is a textured sphere at its true position, distance and orientation, and everything about how it lands on screen follows from measuring it from the eye rather than from the geocenter: parallax is exact at every camera distance, including the ones beyond the Moon's own orbit that the zoom reaches, and so is apparent size, which grows as the camera approaches it. `vs_moon` takes each mesh vertex to world space through one model matrix, subtracts the eye, and maps the resulting direction through the same stereographic sky lens `vs_star` uses; that projection is now one WGSL function (`sky_lens_project`) with two consumers rather than a spelling in each. `fs_moon` is the color texture, a hard terminator (a narrow smoothstep on n dot l against the shared `sun_dir`), an earthshine floor, and a brightness. No water, no fresnel, no night texture.

`scene::moon::place_moon` is the CPU half. It assembles the model matrix from `SkyState`'s position and rotation and the mesh permutation `mesh_from_body`, which puts the Moon's map on the convention the cube's world frame uses, so one orientation serves both bodies: `geometry::sphere` puts the north pole on +Y and the prime meridian on +Z, and `assets::texture_loader::orient`'s flip and quarter shift line an equirectangular map up with that. It also floors the disk in pixels the way `place_sun` floors the Sun's, by inflating the matrix's scale, so the two bodies that subtend the same half degree are the same size on screen wherever the floor is active; `MIN_BODY_DISK_RADIUS_PIXELS` is named for both. Inflating on the CPU keeps the floor to one spelling on one side of the language boundary, which is what the two shared sky-lens rules could not have.

The Moon draws opaque and back-face culled, with no depth write and `CompareFunction::Always`, after the Sun's disk and before the Earth. Opaque because it has to cover the Sun's additive disk; back-face culled because that is exact on a convex sphere and a depth comparison between the sky lens and the Earth's would compare two different projections; before the Earth because the composite's rule is that the painted globe covers whatever falls inside its own disc, which is how a star is hidden too. So clouds and the atmosphere shells sit unambiguously in front of the Moon. Two framings are outside what the composite can show, and they are different kinds of thing. A camera beyond the Moon's orbit, where the Moon is really in front of the Earth, is painted over anyway: that one is given up. A Moon at the antipode of the view axis, which is a camera on the line from the Earth to the Moon at any zoom short of the orbit, is refused instead: the stereographic lens has no finite image of a cone that reaches its antipode, `place_moon` says so by leaving its disc empty, and `write_uniforms` gates the draw on that disc. Without the gate the vertices land thousands of units out in every radial direction at once and the mesh's triangles sweep the frame, opaque and before the Earth; `a_moon_at_the_view_antipode_draws_nothing` is that camera. `vs_moon` carries no offscreen guard of its own, unlike `vs_star`, because a sprite's four vertices share one direction and one verdict moves all of them, where a mesh judged per vertex would keep every triangle that straddles the verdict; the whole mesh is inside the one cone `place_moon` measured, so the CPU gate is exactly sufficient.

It is also a second occluder in `scene::disk_occlusion::visibility`, or the free half of an eclipse would be the wrong half: the Moon covers the Sun's white disk by draw order, and without this the bloom, the corona needles and the halo would go on burning around a dark Moon. Its screen circle comes from the same `sky_lens_disc` at the same floored radius, so unlike the globe comparison this one carries no mixed-lens caveat and agrees with an ephemeris. Where both occluders cover the disk at once the larger hidden area wins rather than their exact union, which is exact everywhere but an eclipse at the limb and errs toward more glare there. A Moon that is not drawn hides nothing, which covers both a switched-off Moon and a missing texture.

Three persisted parameters, all in the Celestial group: `moon_brightness` (zero is the switch, the way `sun_glow` and `star_intensity` are), `moon_size` (1 to 8, a multiplier on the radius and nothing else, 2.5 by default), and `moon_earthshine` (a floor under the unlit face, 0.15 by default so a new moon does not vanish). The honest way to a larger Moon is a narrower `sky_fov`, which magnifies the Moon and the stars around it together; `moon_size` magnifies the Moon alone.

The surface is the NASA CGI Moon Kit at 1024x512 in slot 1, and it is an overlay: excluded from `textures_ready` and `textures_pending` as the clouds are, absent without the file, and never delaying a wallpaper export. The clouds differ in the last: their first answer holds a publish for `CLOUD_WAIT` at the most (architecture.md, The publish). Unlike the clouds its slot is file-backed, so a resolution switch purges and reloads it, and the load is spawned only while `moon_brightness` is above zero. Tests use a generated fixture map (`tests/support`) rather than the LFS asset: the landmarks in it are asymmetric in all three ways the orientation can be wrong, and generating it rather than committing it is what makes the golden reference's bytes reproducible on any machine.

Adding a shader parameter is fourteen edits in seven files:

| File | Edits |
|---|---|
| `ui/main.slint` | the field in its group's settings struct, and its `SettingRow` block with `default-value` |
| `ui_callbacks.rs` | `scene_settings`, `read_params_from_window` |
| `config/mod.rs` | the `AppConfig` field, and its value in `impl Default` |
| `params.rs` | the `SceneParams` field, `from_config`, `write_to_config`, one line of `scene_digest!` |
| `renderer/uniforms.rs` | one line of `uniform_block!` |
| `renderer/render_pass.rs` | `write_uniforms` |
| `shaders/sphere.wgsl` | the `Uniforms` field with its offset comment, and the use |

A color parameter takes the same path with three differences. Its row is a `SettingColor` rather than a `SettingRow`, bound to a `color` field of its settings struct; it is a `color::Rgb8` in `AppConfig` and in `SceneParams`, stored as `#rrggbb`, and `ui_callbacks::slint_color` and `ui_callbacks::rgb8` carry it across the bridge; and it goes in the `colored` group of `scene_digest!`, which compares it exactly and whose mutation row moves its red channel by one. The two ocean colors are the ones there are, and they do not reach the shader as themselves: `write_uniforms` hands it the chosen color less the fill of the floor drawn (The ocean color, above).

The two macro lists are what make the rest safe. `scene_digest!` generates `ParamsDigest`, `digest()` and the mutation table the tests walk from one entry per parameter, and it builds a `SceneParams` out of its own groups in a `const`, so a field that is in the struct and in no group is a missing field the compiler names. `uniform_block!` generates the `Uniforms` struct and a table of its field names, WGSL types and offsets, and two unit tests compare that table against the `Uniforms` block parsed out of `sphere.wgsl`: a name, a type, an offset, a field count or the total size that disagrees fails, and so does a field appended into the trailing padding on one side alone. The bridge in `ui_callbacks.rs` stays hand-written on purpose, since it is the crate's stated translation point, and its struct literals are what make it safe: `scene_settings` writes every settings struct out whole, `apply_params_to_window` destructures the result with no `..`, and `read_params_from_window` reads every group into one literal and builds `SceneParams` as another, so a field or a group missing on either side of the bridge is a compile error. What the compiler cannot see is a `SettingRow` in `main.slint` bound to one field and defaulting from another, which the reset cases in `tests/slint_ui.rs` find without naming any field. A field added to a subsection's struct is covered by that subsection's reset and its group's with no further edit.

A setting that is not a shader parameter takes a different route, and `texture_resolution` is the example: it is an `AppConfig` field with a widget, but it stays out of `SceneParams` and the digest because it does not describe what to draw, and acting on it means re-reading files and swapping GPU textures, which `push_params` cannot express. Such a setting gets its own `EngineCommand` and its own callback, and is read back in `read_config_from_window_onto` rather than in `write_to_config`.

`ParamsDigest` is the quantized snapshot used for dirty checking: camera floats and the colors of the `colored` group compare exactly, every other float is rounded to integer thousandths. `datetime` is deliberately not in the digest; what the shader consumes is the sun direction derived from it, and `FrameState` compares that separately along with the render size and the camera resolved under the frame's sky (The Sun-fixed camera, below). The digest's camera is the one the settings hold, `SceneParams::held_camera`, so the longitude a Sun-fixed camera does not read moves nothing.

## The Moon's shadow

During a solar eclipse the globe, the cloud shell and the Rayleigh shell are drawn with the Moon's shadow on them, in the blend surface mode only. The rule is the share of the Sun's disk the Moon's leaves uncovered, seen from the point drawn, taken as a uniform disk: `scene::eclipse::light_left` on the CPU and `eclipse_light` in `sphere.wgsl`, the fourth rule the shared-rule probe compares. Both work in the frame of the shadow's axis rather than with the angle between the two directions, whose cosine is 1 minus 1e-5 at the umbra's edge and which f32 would resolve to about 130 km on the axis. With `q` the point less the Moon's center and `a` the unit axis from the Sun's center through the Moon's, `z = q . a` and `r = |q - z a|` give the angle between the two centers seen from the point as `atan(r / z) - atan(r / (z + D))`, `D` the Moon to Sun distance, and the two angular radii from the true radii, 1737.4 km and 695,700 km, Astronomy Engine's. The lens area is `disk_occlusion::overlap_area`'s, worked in units of the Sun's radius. Behind the Moon (`z <= 0`) the light is 1.

The point is the true one on the WGS84 ellipsoid, not the point of the drawn sphere. The cube faces put each place's geodetic latitude at the direction whose elevation is that latitude, so the sphere's direction `n` is the true surface normal of the place drawn there, and `surface_point(n, h) = N (n.x, (1 - e^2) n.y, n.z) + h n`, `N = a / sqrt(1 - e^2 n.y^2)`, is where that place is, raised by a shell's height. The globe stays a sphere; only the shadow's geometry is the ellipsoid's, which moves the shadow from the sphere's place, up to 78 km off near the poles, to Astronomy Engine's. The four constants (the two radii, `a` and `e^2`) reach the shader through the uniform block rather than as WGSL constants, so the shader holds no copy to drift. `SkyState::shadow` carries the Moon's center, the axis and `D` for each frame's own instant, the axis taken in f64 from the Sun's vector before it is normalized.

**The gate.** `render_pass::draws_eclipse` opens only in the blend mode with `moon_shadow` above zero and while the penumbral cone reaches the sphere of `RAYLEIGH_RADIUS`, the outermost shell, by the cone's distance from the Earth's center (`eclipse::penumbra_reaches`). Then it sets bit 5 of `flags`, `FLAG_ECLIPSE`, and fills the eclipse fields; otherwise they are zero. Each of `fs_main`, `fs_cloud` and `fs_rayleigh` calls `eclipse_shade` once, which tests the flag in uniform control flow and returns exactly 1 outside it, and a product with an exact 1 is the value it multiplies, so every frame outside an eclipse, and every frame in the grid, Day or Night mode, is the frame it was before the shadow existed: `outside_its_gate_the_shadow_changes_no_pixel` compares such frames byte for byte, and the golden references did not move.

**From light to pixels.** The shade is `mix(1, pow(light, 1 / 2.2), moon_shadow)`, the light re-encoded as the stored texels are, so half the light draws at 0.73 and a hundredth at 0.12, the inner half of the penumbra visibly darker and a dark core. It multiplies every term that is sunlight: the day surface in `blend_fragment_lit` after its `min(night, day)` clamp, which would otherwise hold the eclipsed day up at the night texture (`blend_fragment` is the same with the factor at 1, which is what `tests/shading.rs` runs), the specular glint and the Fresnel sky color in `fs_main`, the day brightness and the share of the deck drawn at the day opacity in `fs_cloud`, so a deck under the umbra goes dark at the night opacity, and the day blue and the sunrise lobe in `fs_rayleigh`. The nightglow shells do not take it, and the umbra shows no night texture, so the wanted set of tiles is the same as without the shadow. Limb darkening, which would make the deep partial phase a quarter to a half darker, is left for the look to ask for (plan research section 3.2).

**What it costs.** Inside the gate every fragment of the three draws evaluates the rule once: two `atan`, two `asin`, the two `acos` of the lens and a few square roots. Measured on 2026-10-29 on the development desktop (Ryzen 7 5800X, Radeon RX 6800 XT), release build, by `measure_what_the_shadow_costs_a_preview_frame` (an ignored case in `tests/engine/eclipse.rs`), as the time from an `UpdateParams` to the preview frame, with the camera over the 2024-04-08 peak point at the default zoom, blend mode over the Earth fixture with the cloud fixture, the Moon and the Milky Way, the High tier, 25 pairs after 5 of the same instant with the gate closed and open: WARP 53.6 against 56.4 ms at 704 by 576, 113.7 against 119.3 ms at 1088 by 896 and 213.3 against 217.4 ms at 1920 by 1088, so 2 to 5 percent, and the Radeon nothing it can resolve (0.8, 1.2 and 2.1 to 2.3 ms either way). The budget proposed in the plan is a tenth of a WARP frame.

**Where it lands.** At the peaks of 2024-04-08 and 2026-08-12, the second with its shadow near the polar limb, the centroid of the pixels the shadow leaves least light in, reprojected through the camera, is 0.36 and 0.30 km from Astronomy Engine's peak point with 4.4 km pixels (`the_umbra_falls_where_the_library_puts_the_peak`); at the annular peak of 2023-10-14 the antumbra's center keeps a third of its brightness and the penumbra 80 pixels out more (`the_antumbras_center_is_lit_and_darker_than_the_penumbra`).

## The Milky Way


The diffuse band is a panorama of the whole celestial sphere sampled per pixel, drawn first in the pass so everything else in the sky sits on it. `vs_milky_way` is the four-vertex screen quad the sun draws generate from `vertex_index`, and it is always the whole frame: the sky lens has an image of every direction short of the antipode, the antipode is past the frame's corner at every field of view the slider offers, and so there is no region to leave undrawn and nothing here to cull. `MilkyWay::select` is the only gate, on the intensity and on whether the texture has arrived, which is `Stars::select`'s shape and not the Moon's: a fullscreen quad has no geometry that can fail to appear, so nothing about the frame can narrow it.

`fs_milky_way` inverts the chain a star sprite goes through. `milky_way_direction` calls phase B's `sky_lens_direction` on the framebuffer position and then transposes the view matrix and `world_from_eqj`, which makes it the exact inverse of `sky_lens_project` after `view_from_eqj`, the forward composition factored out of `vs_star` when this became its second consumer. That pairing is what puts the panorama at the same scale and orientation as the sprites on top of it, and it is three places a sign can be wrong, so `the_panoramas_reconstruction_inverts_the_projection_it_sits_under` in `tests/render_pipeline.rs` is a compute entry point appended to the production shaders that feeds directions through the forward pair and back, over both ends of the field of view and both signs of pan: the round trip holds to 6.5e-7 of chord distance on `warp` and 1.5e-4 on `lavapipe`, which is half a hundredth of a degree and is the two adapters' transcendentals rather than anything about the chain. The tolerance of 1e-3 sits an order of magnitude above the worse of them and three below the faults it exists to catch, which are 1.229 and 0.546 with either transpose dropped.

`milky_way_uv` is the panorama's own layout, and the one constant in it is `PANORAMA_RIGHT_ASCENSION_ZERO`. The asset is a standard astronomical all-sky map (right ascension zero at the center, increasing to the left, north up, which is the opposite handedness from the Earth's and the Moon's maps because a sphere seen from inside runs the other way round from one seen from outside), and `assets::texture_loader::orient` mirrors and quarter-shifts every equirectangular source it loads. The mirror is what turns right ascension the right way round for this map and the shift is what moves its zero a quarter of the way across, so the shader undoes the shift and nothing else. `textures/PROVENANCE.md` records the measurement that this is the source's layout, against eleven sky positions, because the SVS does not document it and the two readings differ by a mirror that the galactic center alone cannot tell apart.

The wrap is handled with explicit gradients rather than patched later. `atan2` jumps a full turn across its branch cut, so a hardware derivative of `u` there is a whole texture width and the sampler answers that column with the coarsest mip: the average of the entire panorama, drawn as a curve from pole to pole. The direction is continuous across the cut, so `milky_way_uv_gradient` carries its derivative through the map by the chain rule and `textureSampleGrad` takes the result. `the_wrap_column_is_not_a_band_of_the_coarsest_mip` is what holds it, and two things about that case are worth knowing before editing it. Its fixture is bands of declination rather than a gradient, because a linear ramp is a fixed point of the mip chain and a case built on one passed with the gradient sample deleted. And its metric is a second difference over the sky pixels rather than a per-column count, because the cut is a curve on screen and two pixels wide, a derivative being a property of the fragment quad.

What the layer costs is seven transcendentals per pixel over the whole frame, which is negligible on a real adapter and is not on a software one: measured, the panorama adds 0.2 ms to a 1920 by 1080 frame and 0.9 ms to a 4K one on this machine's GPU, and 133 ms and 555 ms on `warp`, against the cloud shell's 16 ms at 1080p. It is not the texture fetch, which the sampler's anisotropy makes no difference to. Nothing in the suite pays it except the three goldens and the panorama engine cases, because every other headless configuration leaves the slot empty; the soak test's seven simulated days draw no panorama at all. The plan's departure 6 has the whole table and names the algebraic identity that would remove three of the seven from `sky_lens_direction`, which is not taken because that function is the Sun's too and substituting it moves every reference.

The layer's slot is the second file-backed one, slot 2, and the only texture whose source width sits between two of the caps, so `memory::milky_way_texture_bytes` is 42.7 MiB at the 8192 and 4096 settings and 10.7 MiB at 2048, where the load halves it in memory. It is an overlay, like the clouds and the Moon, so `textures_ready` and `textures_pending` exclude it and a checkout without the Git LFS object draws a sky without a band rather than waiting for one.

Three goldens pin it, `panorama_behind_the_stars`, `panorama_at_a_narrow_sky` and `panorama_at_a_wide_sky`, the same night-side camera at the two ends of the field-of-view slider, both with the banded fixture rather than the real asset. `base_params` switches the layer off for every other case: it covers the whole frame, so leaving it on would move every other reference and bury what each of them is for. What a fixture cannot show is the asset's own layout, and `the_real_panorama_has_the_galactic_plane_where_the_plane_is` in `tests/engine/` is where that lives, sampling the rendered sky at the galactic center, both galactic poles and two stretches of the plane and asserting the ordering a mirrored reading inverts. Its sibling `no_bright_star_is_baked_into_the_real_panorama` holds the other property of the file itself, that the layer excludes the bright stars the sprites draw: on the asset as it sits on disk, at the position of every catalog record inside magnitude 1.3, a 3x3 texel core against the 41x41 window around it reads 1.26 at worst against a bound of two, where a star baked into the layer would saturate its texels and read 2.3 to 5. Both skip with a printed reason without the Git LFS object.

### What the panorama test can and cannot see

`no_bright_star_is_baked_into_the_real_panorama` compares the mean of a 3x3 texel window at a catalog star's own position against the mean of the 41x41 window around it and bounds the ratio at 2. What that window cannot see is a star confined to a single texel in the brightest part of the plane: raising one texel of the nine to 765 where the sky already reads 363, which is the brightest core in the set, takes the core to 408 and the ratio to 1.26, inside the bound. Taking the peak texel of the core rather than its mean does not fix that, and was measured: the map's own grain already puts single texels at 2.08 times the local mean, so a peak metric has no separation left to spend.

## Clouds on the night side

`fs_cloud` shades the shell rather than the ground, and the difference is three things. Its
ramp is centered on the shell's own tangent condition, `sqrt(1 - 1/r^2)`, which at
`CLOUD_SPHERE_RADIUS` is 0.0547 and puts the cloud terminator 3.14 degrees nightward of the
globe's, the 9.6 km of altitude being what keeps a cloud top in the direct beam that much
longer; `fs_rayleigh` computes the same expression for its own limb as `earth_limb_ndotv`. Its
width is `params::CLOUD_TERMINATOR_WIDTH`, a constant at 0.18 rather than a slider, wider than
the globe's 0.1 because twilight goes on lighting a cloud top for several degrees after the
beam has gone. That constant is also the sentinel fix: `write_uniforms` puts -1.0 in
`terminator_width` outside blend mode, as the flag `fs_main` reads to ignore the sun, and
`fs_cloud` used to read the same uniform, which reversed its `smoothstep` edges in the three
single-texture modes. Whether the shell is lit by the Sun at all is a separate question, carried
in `flags` rather than in the sentinel, because the grid carries the sentinel too and keeps its
clouds under the Sun: Day and Night light the deck evenly, see The Day and Night surfaces.

The night value is `cloud_night`, 0.20 by default, and it is an appearance parameter rather
than an irradiance. The plan's own finding is why: the day side is about 18.6 stops brighter
than the night side, by the sun-to-full-moon ratio and independently by the exposure settings
of "Hello, World" against those of "The Blue Marble", which is more than any sensor or eye
holds at once, so the frame is a tone map and the number is a decision about how much of that
gap to compress. It is chosen against the texture it draws over: Black Marble's unlit land
reads 0.137, its Antarctica and a typical city cluster 0.20, the Nile delta 0.36 and its cores
1.0, so 0.20 puts a night cloud above unlit land, level with Antarctica and the faintest
clusters, and well below the lights.

Each hemisphere carries its own opacity, and only the day one is a multiplier on coverage.
`cloud_opacity` is that multiplier and is unchanged. `cloud_opacity_night`, 0.45 by default, is
read as an optical depth instead, `t / (1 - t)` clamped by `NIGHT_OPACITY_MIN_TRANSMITTANCE` to
at most 32, and `alpha = 1 - pow(1 - density, depth)`. Two things forced that. The same alpha
does not read the same on the two sides: a deck at `cloud_night` is darker than the city under
it and far darker than a core at display white, so the surface wins a blend that on the day
side it loses invisibly. And the source cannot supply the density a multiplier would need,
because the published composite's median texel is 0.79 and only half a percent of it reaches
0.97, which through the floor and gamma is a density of 0.45 for a cloud that looks solid. So a
straight multiply at 100 percent left a third of the ground showing, which is what
`the_night_opacity_reaches_full_cover` measures: over the fixture's city the window read 246.7
with the deck off, 164.7 at the default and 89.0 at the top of the range, against the 89.2 the
deck alone is worth, where the straight multiply reads 175.3 at the top. Those were taken with
`cloud_night` at 0.35 and the night opacity at 0.55; at today's defaults on WARP it reads 200.7,
124.7 and 51.0, against a deck of 51.0. The base of the `pow`
is held off zero because WGSL computes it as `exp2(y * log2(x))`, so `pow(0, 0)`, which is a
fully dense cloud at zero night opacity, is a NaN. `SceneParams::draws_clouds` is the draw gate, the "Show clouds" switch with either opacity up, since one
hemisphere at zero is not the layer switched off.

Two references pin it, and not interchangeably: `clouds_across_the_terminator` holds the floor
and `cloud_terminator_close_up` holds the shift and the width. Neither holds the night opacity:
their cloud fixture is 255 or nothing, so every cloud pixel in it has a density of one and any
nonzero night opacity covers the ground completely. `FixtureClouds::uniform` is the map at one
mid value that the opacity case uses instead. They are the suite's blend-mode goldens, drawn
over the golden engine's cube of the real Earth, which is why `check_golden_in` waits for
`day_floor`, `night_floor` and `water_mask`: the transcoder builds their packs after the
engine starts, and a case that did not wait would export the frame the grid draws.

## Haze removal, the latitude and the land weight

The cloud map is mostly an infrared temperature map, so cold clear ground reads as a grey veil, and how grey depends on the region: near zero over tropical ocean and around 0.8 over mid-latitude land in October, with real cloud as little as 0.08 above it ([plans/2026-10-10-cloud-haze-research.md](plans/2026-10-10-cloud-haze-research.md), sections 1 to 4). One global floor cannot take that off, so `fs_cloud` subtracts a local one: the map's own clear-sky level, estimated per map, at a strength the user sets toward the equator and toward the poles, with the floor and the gamma applied after it, each in a land and an ocean value. The plan is [plans/2026-10-10-cloud-haze-plan.md](plans/2026-10-10-cloud-haze-plan.md).

**The estimate.** `assets::cloud_haze::background` turns a decoded one-channel map into a 512 x 256 one-channel image, about 78 km a pixel at the equator, in four steps: an area average down to that grid, which accepts any input size because `SUNLIT_EARTH_CLOUD_URL` can serve one that is no published variant; the 15th percentile over a 9 x 9 window, about 700 km, the share of a window taken to be clear sky; and a Gaussian blur of sigma 3, about 230 km, truncated at four sigma. The level is stored as measured, up to full brightness; the cap at 0.85 belongs to `fs_cloud`, since a haze removal above 1 lifts it. The percentile is the rank scipy's percentile filter takes, 12 of 81 counting from 0, read on the map's 8-bit levels from a histogram that slides along each row, so a step costs the two columns that change rather than a sort of the window. Every filter wraps across the antimeridian and clamps at the poles. `CloudUpdater::post` runs it on the cloud worker's thread before it posts either image, so the cached map and every download get one and the engine thread never sees the work; the log line `estimated the clear-sky background` carries the time. On this machine the 8192 x 4096 cached map takes 11.9 ms in a release build (median of eleven, 11.8 at the fastest) and 444 ms in a debug build (379 at the fastest), against the plan's bound of 100 ms in release. None of the four numbers is a setting: they define the estimate rather than taste, and changing one means recomputing it.

**The land weight.** `fs_cloud` reads the water mask cube, 255 over open water, at the level whose faces are 128 px wide, about 80 km a texel, which is how soft a coastline the settings change across. The level is `log2(width / 128)` of the bound mask's own size, level 3 of the shipped 1024 px faces, so the 8 px mask of the test fixture is read at its finest. It is an explicit level and not an explicit gradient, so it does not reach the `gradient2d` restriction that keeps the globe off `textureSampleGrad` on Metal, and `metal_is_never_asked_for_a_cube_gradient` still translates the whole shader. Where no mask is resident the cloud group binds the dummy cube, which reads zero, so every pixel is land: a session without the cube textures, and the preview before the mask's pack lands. Every mode that draws from the cube reads the mask itself since the ocean colors (The ocean color, above), so a Day, Night or blend publish waits for it whether the cloud layer is drawn or not. Sea ice is water to the mask, so frozen sea takes the ocean's floor and gamma. Looked at under a March cloud map over Hudson Bay and the Barents and Kara Seas, the clouds brighten the ice by a mean of 8 to 11 levels, about what they add to the snowy land beside it, and the land's values would take 3 to 5 of those off; what they leave is faint streaks of thin cloud over the pack near the terminator. Taking the ice as land by the day floor's brightness, as the water term does, is a possible refinement and not done ([plans/2026-10-11-baked-sea-ice-plan.md](plans/2026-10-11-baked-sea-ice-plan.md), departure 14).

**The formula.** The floor and the gamma are blended from their ocean value toward their land value by `land`, through `by_land(ocean, land_value, land)`, which is `mix` written as `ocean + (land_value - ocean) * land` so that two equal values come back exactly. The haze removal is blended by latitude instead, the same over land and water. What a strength takes off is `haze_removed(haze, background)`: up to 1, that share of the background up to the cap, `haze * min(background, 0.85)`; above 1 the cap is lifted instead, `cap = 0.85 + (haze - 1) * 0.15`, which reaches full brightness at 2, and it takes `min(background, cap)`, never more than the estimate, only more of it where the ground reads above 0.85. The amount is worked out for the equatorward and for the poleward strength and the two are blended, `mix(haze_removed(equator), haze_removed(pole), smoothstep(latitude - band / 2, latitude + band / 2, |phi|))`, with `phi` from `asin(n.y)` in degrees. Blending the two strengths instead finished the change early in the band whenever one was above 1, since an amount above 1 stops growing once the lifted cap passes the level: with 100 and 200 percent over ground at 0.92, the cap passed it nearly half of the way through the strengths, at 147 percent, and the rest of the band showed no change (plan departure 11). By a rough block count on the October map, the 15th percentile of blocks of about 700 km, 39 to 100 percent of the blocks in each band of 10 degrees poleward of 60 read above the cap, 3 to 56 percent between 40 and 60, and at most 7 percent equatorward of 40, so a value above 1 acts mostly where the cap left a veil. The cap stays at 0.85 for a haze of 1 or less because the inside of a deck wider than the window reads as ground to the percentile, and real cloud over mid-latitude land reads about 0.87. Taking the background off and then the floor is one subtraction of `local_floor = floor + removed * (1 - floor)`, which is `1 - (1 - removed) * (1 - floor)` written so that no haze leaves the floor as it is; `floored = saturate((raw - local_floor) / (1 - local_floor))` and `density = pow(floored, 1 / gamma)` follow as before. So with both haze values at 0 and the land and ocean values equal the layer is drawn exactly as it was with one floor and one gamma. Everything after the density, the lighting through `shell_n_dot_l`, the terminator and the night opacity, is unchanged and applies to both surfaces. The background is sampled with the cloud sampler at the map's own `uv`, so it wraps where the map does.

**The settings.** The panel's Clouds group has a "Show clouds" checkbox, `clouds_enabled`, and under it the three shared sliders (day opacity, night opacity, night brightness), then a Haze removal heading with "Toward the equator" and "Toward the poles", 0 to 200 percent each, "Switch latitude", 20 to 80 degrees, and "Blend width", 5 to 90 degrees, and a Land and an Ocean heading with "Floor" and "Gamma" each. The haze removal starts at 90 percent toward the equator and 130 toward the poles, a cap of 0.895 there, switching at 55 degrees over a band of 25, all from the user's tuning in the running app; land at a floor of 25 percent and a gamma of 0.5, ocean at a floor of 10 percent and a gamma of 0.75. Haze removal had a land and an ocean value first, like the floor and the gamma. The user found different strengths for land and ocean looked strange where the floors and gammas did not, and that the cap left a veil near the poles that no strength up to 100 percent reached, which is what the split by latitude and the range above 100 percent are for (plan departure 10). The exponent is `1 / gamma`, so a gamma below one pushes the thin cloud down and keeps the dense tops, and one above it lifts the thin cloud; the slider's hint said the reverse until this change. `clouds_enabled` is not a uniform: it is in `scene_digest!`'s `switched` group and in `draws_clouds`, which is now `clouds_enabled && (cloud_opacity > 0 || cloud_opacity_night > 0)`. Off, nothing is drawn and no new download or decode starts: a transfer already under way is not interrupted, and what it brings back is dropped undecoded (architecture.md, The engine).

**What it costs to draw.** Two more texture reads per cloud fragment, one of them a cube at a fixed level. Measured on `warp` at 1920 by 1080 as the difference between an export with the clouds and the same export with both opacities at zero, alternated 60 times after three warm-up pairs and taken as the median of the pairwise differences, over Day mode on the cube surface built from `textures/` with the cached 8192 map, in a release test build: the shader before this change costs 26.4 to 33.7 ms in four runs (mean 30.4), and after it 30.3 to 36.3 ms in five (mean 33.6), so about 3 ms or a tenth more, within the run-to-run spread and well under the quarter the plan set as the threshold for a finding. This method gives the old cloud shell about 30 ms where the Milky Way section above and the roadmap record 16 ms at 1080p; the scene and the harness behind the 16 ms are not recorded, so the two figures should not be compared with each other. Lavapipe was not measured, because this machine reaches it only through WSL.

**What is left to judge.** A deck crossing a coast, which changes how its floor and gamma are drawn over about 80 km, for which the mask level is the knob.

**The goldens.** `clouds_across_the_terminator` and `cloud_terminator_close_up` draw the banded fixture, 255 or 0, over the Earth fixture's mask. Inside a band the background is 255, of which at most about 0.89 comes off at the defaults, which leaves 255 at full density, so the bands themselves stay and what moved is small; testing.md has the figures.

## The Sun-fixed camera

The world frame is Earth-fixed: +Y is the north pole, +Z is 0N 0E and +X is 0N 90E, and `OrbitalCamera` hangs the eye at a longitude, a latitude and a distance with world +Y as up. The sky turns through that frame, the stars once a sidereal day and the Sun once a solar day. The Sun-fixed mode (`SceneParams::sun_fixed`, "Fixed toward the Sun" in Camera Position) keeps the camera's place against the Sun instead, so the Earth turns beneath it once a solar day and the phase it shows stays the same. The frame it holds is the Earth-fixed one turned about +Y by the Sun's longitude and about nothing else: latitude stays a latitude on the Earth, up stays north, and tilt, yaw and pitch mean what they mean in the Earth-fixed mode. What still moves is the Sun's declination, 23.44 degrees either side of the equator over the year and at most about 0.4 degrees a day, so the terminator slides north and south with the seasons. An ecliptic frame would hold the Sun exactly still, at the cost of the Earth's axis nodding 23.4 degrees toward and away from the camera over the year with every orientation setting changing its meaning; the plan has the argument (`plans/2026-10-16-sun-fixed-camera-plan.md`, decisions 1 and 2).

The camera's place is a time of day, `solar_time`, the local apparent solar time beneath it in hours: 12:00 hangs it over the meridian the Sun stands over, 18:00 over the evening terminator, 00:00 over the middle of the night. With `λs` the Sun's longitude, `atan2(sun.x, sun.z)` in the world frame (`scene::camera::subsolar_longitude`), `longitude_of` and `solar_time_of` convert between the two:

```
longitude  = wrap180(λs + (solar_time - 12) * 15)
solar_time = wrap24(12 + (longitude - λs) / 15)
```

The Sun's actual longitude is used, not a mean one, so the Sun stands at the same hour angle all year; mean solar time would let the lighting wander by up to 16 minutes, about 4 degrees, with the equation of time. A turn of the globe is one apparent solar day, which differs from 24 hours by at most about half a minute.

Nothing in the shader changes. `SceneParams::camera_at(sky)` is the one place the camera is resolved: the settings' camera in the Earth-fixed mode, and that camera with the longitude above in the Sun-fixed mode. `write_uniforms` draws with it under the sky it is handed, the engine's `tile_output` builds every output of the wanted set from it under the sky that output is drawn under, and `FrameState` compares it exactly, so the picture, the tiles and the dirty check agree on which side of the Earth is in view and the residency and `OrbitalCamera::from_params` see Earth-fixed cameras only. A publish draws once and replays the sky the renderer then holds for every shot, so the screens of one publish resolve at one instant and a spanned picture still meets at the seams. The sky state is computed to the whole second, so the resolved camera moves in steps of a 240th of a degree, under half a pixel at the closest zoom on a 1080 pixel tall preview.

**Keeping the preview up with the turn.** The surface beneath the camera crosses the frame at about `focal * ω / (d - 1)` pixels a second, with `focal = height / (2 * tan(camera_fov / 2))`, ω the Earth's turn against the Sun and d the camera's distance in Earth radii. At the default 20 degree lens on a 1080 pixel tall frame:

| Distance | Pixels per second | One pixel every | Step at 2 min | Step at a 5 min refresh |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 (default) | 0.032 | 31 s | 4 px | 10 px |
| 3.2 (Europe) | 0.10 | 10 s | 12 px | 31 px |
| 1.5 (closest) | 0.45 | 2.2 s | 53 px | 134 px |

So with a Sun-fixed camera at live time and the preview shown, the sky schedule's interval is the time the preview target takes to move one pixel, by that formula with the target's own height and the camera's own lens and distance, clamped between `SKY_FLOOR`, 5 seconds, and `SKY_INTERVAL`, two minutes (`engine::sky_interval`). It is worked out again on `UpdateParams`, `SetPreviewSize` and `SetPreviewEnabled`, and a change restarts the countdown from then. Hidden, in the Earth-fixed mode, or with the date and time overridden, it stays at two minutes, because a hidden window still draws on that schedule and has nobody to show the frame to.

The floor was measured on 2026-10-18 on the development desktop, a Ryzen 7 5800X with a Radeon RX 6800 XT, with the engine at `opt-level = 2`: the time from an `UpdateParams` that moves the time of day to the `PreviewFrame` it produces, so the sky state, the wanted set, the draw and the readback, in blend mode over the Earth fixture's cube and its tiles with the cloud fixture, the real Moon and Milky Way, the High tier's MSAA and the Europe preset, the median of 25 frames after 5 to warm up. The Slint side's upload and paint of the frame is not in it.

| Preview | WARP | Radeon RX 6800 XT (Vulkan) |
|---|---|---|
| 704 by 576, about the default window at 100% scaling | 79 ms | 1.3 ms |
| 1088 by 896 | 172 ms | 2.2 ms |
| 1920 by 1088 | 324 ms | 3.9 ms |

The floor binds only at the closest zooms, below about 2.1 radii on a 1080 pixel tall preview and about 1.6 on the default one, and there it costs WARP 1.6% of the time at the default preview and 6.5% at a 1080 pixel one. A GPU could take a floor of a second for a fraction of a percent, but 5 seconds still keeps the steps to about 2 pixels at the closest zoom on a preview up to 1080 pixels tall, so one floor serves every adapter.

The wallpaper keeps its own auto-refresh interval: the last column of the first table is what this mode shows on the desktop at the five minute default, and a shorter interval is the user's to choose ([usage.md](usage.md)).

## One view across several screens

Producing a wallpaper is the most expensive thing the engine does: a render at the display's native resolution, then a readback of that whole image, which is about 14 MB at 2560x1440 on a CPU rasterizer where there is no hardware to help. That is why `check_supported` is asked before anything is rendered rather than after: a sink that is going to refuse the work has to say so before the cost is paid.

`display::layout` turns a list of monitors into the renders a wallpaper publish makes. Everything in it is a pure function of rectangles, so the whole model is exercised on a machine with one screen; what the sections below record is the geometry, and [architecture.md](architecture.md) has the publish path around it.

Two lenses, and the renderer does not anchor them to the same axis:

- The Earth lens is `Mat4::perspective_rh(fov_y, aspect, ..)`. Its scale in pixels per unit of tan-space is `height / (2 * tan(camera_fov / 2))` and does not depend on the width at all.
- The sky lens is stereographic and anchored the other way. `sky_lens_project` builds NDC as `radial * r / edge * vec2(1, aspect)`, which puts the frame's *horizontal* edge at `tan(sky_fov / 4)`, so its scale is `width / (2 * tan(sky_fov / 4))` and does not depend on the height.
- The pan is in NDC, so it stretches with the frame on both axes.

Fed the settings directly, that made a sky direction's distance from the globe, in globe radii, proportional to the frame's width over its height. A 4:3 preview drew the Moon 25% closer to the globe than a 16:9 wallpaper did, a 3440x1440 ultrawide magnified the sky 1.34 times against a 16:9 screen, and since the preview, an export and a wallpaper each framed the scene their own way, a portrait CLI render did not even match a portrait wallpaper ([plan](plans/2026-10-24-short-edge-framing-plan.md)).

**The short edge rule.** The settings measure both fields of view across the picture's short edge, and the pan in halves of it. Every output is then the same picture, uniformly scaled so the short edges match and centered on the same point, and outputs differ only in how far they reach along the long edge. `display::layout::output_framing` is the one translation from the settings to the units the shader takes. An output is a width, a height and a reference short edge `S` in its own pixels, and its lenses are:

- `camera_fov_out = 2 * atan(tan(camera_fov / 2) * height / S)`, vertical;
- `sky_fov_out = 4 * atan(tan(sky_fov / 4) * width / S)`, horizontal;
- `offset_ndc_x = offset_x * S / width` and `offset_ndc_y = offset_y * S / height`.

The principal point stays at the output's center, and the shader, the uniform block and `write_uniforms` are untouched: they still take a vertical Earth lens, a horizontal sky lens and an NDC pan, now derived per output. The type system holds every path to it: `write_uniforms`, `Renderer::render`, `export_image_capped` and the wanted set's `tile_output` take `OutputParams`, which only `OutputFraming::applied_to` builds. A screen and an export of any size take `S = min(width, height)` (`screen_framing`), a span starts from its anchor's (below), and the preview takes the rule of its own (below).

Worked cases at the defaults, one image per screen. A 1920x1080 notebook and a 3440x1440 ultrawide put the globe in the same place at the same fraction of the height, and the Sun and the Moon keep their places relative to it; the ultrawide shows 0.31 screen heights more sky on either side, about 173 degrees across against 140. A scene composed on a 3440x1440 screen and rendered at 16:9 is the same picture at the same height with the outer 13% of the width cut on either side: a body placed there is not in the render, and a far panned globe can be cut, but nothing moves. `every_output_is_the_same_picture_in_short_edges` in `display::layout` holds the rule as a property over random screens, panels and exports, through `sky_lens_disc` and `globe_screen_circle`.

**The contain rule is a case of it.** On a landscape output the short edge is the height, so the Earth lens is the setting itself. On a portrait one it is the width, which widens the Earth lens until the horizontal extent is what the vertical one is at square: the globe is contained rather than run off the sides, which is what the separate contain rule did before. The sky and the horizontal pan are what changed, and config version 4 converts both for the 16:9 screen most files were tuned on (`config::migrate::v3_to_v4`): the old default of 140 across the width becomes the new default of 86 across the height, which draws 140.01 across a 16:9 width, any other value its exact conversion, and `offset_x` is scaled by 16/9.

**The preview.** The anchor screen's rectangle is always as tall as the preview frame: `S = frame_height * min(1, A)`, where `A` is the anchor screen's width over its height (`layout::preview_short_edge`). With a landscape screen that is the frame's height whatever the panel's shape, so a wide panel shows more sky to the sides and a narrow one clips the screen's sides rather than shrinking the picture. With a portrait screen the rectangle's width is `frame_height * A`, so it fits the panel's height too. Height decides the scale and width only shows or hides the sides. With no screen known, a headless engine or a session the platform cannot enumerate, `S` is the frame's height. The anchor is the one a wallpaper plan uses, `resolve_anchor` over the engine's last monitor list and the stored anchor, and `S` is computed from the frame the engine actually renders, after the tier cap. `the_preview_puts_the_globe_where_an_export_of_the_screen_does` compares the globe's centroid and radius in short edges between the preview and a 1600x900 export, in a panel wider than the screen, one narrower, and with no screen; they agree to a few ten thousandths of a short edge.

The frame guides (the camera panel's frame button, or F) draw the anchor screen's, 16:9's and 4:3's rectangles over the preview with their short side the displayed short edge. Each `PreviewFrame` carries the short edge it was framed on and the screen it was framed for, and the window computes the displayed length under `image-fit: cover` from them, so the guides follow the frame on screen, including an old frame shown while a resize settles. The slow drag gain is measured on the same length.

**The lens clamps.** The derived sky lens is clamped to 1 to 330 degrees, `layout::SKY_FOV_MIN` and `SKY_FOV_MAX`, with `sphere.wgsl` and `scene::sky_lens` clamping to the same numbers. The lower end was 60 while the setting was the horizontal lens itself; a narrow output now legitimately derives less, down to 7.5 degrees for the narrowest slider position on a 1:4 preview panel, so its only job is keeping the tangent off zero. The derived Earth lens is held below 179 degrees, reachable only with a setting near the slider's 170 on a strongly portrait output. `OutputFraming` reports either clamp: a wallpaper publish adds it to its status note and an export logs a warning.

**The span derivation.** Let the anchor be `A` and the canvas `C` be the bounding box of every monitor. The requirement is that the canvas render, cropped to the anchor's rectangle, is the render the anchor would have got alone, and a projection with a constant scale in tan-space reduces that to keeping pixels per unit of tan-space equal and moving the principal point. The anchor's own `screen_framing` is the base, so each lens is in the units the shader takes, and each is scaled on the axis the shader takes it on:

- `camera_fov_canvas = 2 * atan(tan(camera_fov / 2) * C.height / A.height)`
- `sky_fov_canvas = 4 * atan(tan(sky_fov / 4) * C.width / A.width)`
- `offset_x_canvas = -nx + offset_x * A.width / C.width`, and the same in y, where `(nx, ny)` is the anchor's center in canvas NDC. The first term moves the principal point onto the anchor; the second carries the anchor's own pan, which is in the anchor's NDC, into the canvas's.

For the common case of screens side by side at equal height the Earth lens is untouched, since `C.height / A.height` is 1: the canvas is simply wider and the globe in the anchor's crop is exactly the standalone render. The sky lens is not untouched there, because `C.width / A.width` is 2 or more.

**The sky's ceiling is 330 degrees.** The slider means the short edge and every output extends outward from it, so a 16:9 screen draws 52.7 to 183 degrees across from the slider's 30 to 120, and a canvas derives a wider sky still: two equal 16:9 screens at the default, 140 degrees across one of them, come to 218. The lens is stereographic with edge radius `tan(sky_fov / 4)` and goes singular only at 360, where the antipode maps to infinity; nothing happens at 180, where the edge radius is exactly 1 and the frame corner sits 98 degrees off the view axis on a 16:9 screen. What is linear in canvas pixels is that tangent rather than the angle, so the derived value self-limits as screens are added: three screens at the default are 258 degrees, eight are 320, a thousand are 359.7. `SKY_FOV_MAX` is 330, which clears a seven-wide span at the widest slider position and about eleven at the default, and leaves the frame corner 13.1 degrees clear of the antipode. `sphere.wgsl` and `scene::sky_lens` clamp to the same number and `the_shader_and_the_cpu_agree_on_the_three_shared_rules` walks 0.5, 330 and 400 to keep them from drifting apart. `OutputFraming::sky_clamped` still reports a canvas past it, and the publish says so in the status line, but no layout built out of screens that fit on a desk reaches it.

Above 180 degrees three things were never exercised before this, and none of them is known to be wrong: the Sun's glare composition, which is angular and was measured to 180; the star sprites, which carry the conformal `(1 + r * r)` factor and magnify hard toward the corners; and the Milky Way, sampled through the lens inverse and stretched severely at a corner 167 degrees off the view axis. `golden_panorama_at_a_wide_sky` renders all three at 300 degrees on the software adapter, which is what turns them from claims into a reference a change has to preserve.

**What does not continue across the seam.** Where the canvas is taller than the anchor, star sprites do not match the standalone render: `sphere.wgsl` sizes them in pixels against `viewport_size` and scales them by `clamp(viewport_size.y / 1080, 1, 2)`, so a taller canvas draws slightly different stars. The globe, the atmosphere, the Sun and the Milky Way all follow the tan-space scaling and do match. That is a departure rather than a bug, and it is what the two GPU cases in `tests/engine/` assert: equal heights compare the anchor's crop against its standalone render at the golden suite's tolerance with nothing excluded from the scene, and unequal heights compare only the globe's centroid and radius.
